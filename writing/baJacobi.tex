	\section{Introduction}
	Artificial Intelligence can be of great assistance in scientific research \autocite{aiHistWang}. As shown by \citeauthor{aiUseStat} \autocite{aiUseStat}, researchers in natural science are among the groups most familiar with \gls{AI} compared to other scientists. There is a wide range of applications, stretching from finding sources in the literature to aiding in the presenting research findings. \gls{AI} can be useful for forming hypothesis and evaluating them by designing and controlling practical experiments and aiding during simulations, especially with the program implementation. \autocite{aiHistWang}
	
	In the past two years automated \gls{Agentic AI} research pipelines where developed with the potential to improve the work with \gls{AI} in science. The first fully autonomous approach was published by \citeauthor{aiScientist} in \citedate{aiScientist} \autocite{aiScientist}, introducing a framework called \enquote{The AI Scientist} \autocite{aiScientist}. It identifies interesting research questions, plans and executes according experiments, interprets the results, and writes a paper stating its findings via a multistep process. A similar system is the \enquote{InternAgent} \autocite{internAgent}, which provides an interface for introducing human feedback into the system. This is realised by the \enquote{Orchestration Agent} \autocite{internAgent} managing how and when human input is introduced into the system. The system itself consists of the four distinct tasks of \enquote{Survey}, \enquote{Code Review}, \enquote{Assessment}, and \enquote{Idea Innovation} \autocite{internAgent}. The \enquote{Orchestration Agent} \autocite{internAgent} manages the interactions between the tasks and how the findings are presented to the human. This design shows some resemblance to human research groups with individuals specialized in specific aspects of the work while a supervisor coordinates the process. In tests ran by the \enquote{InternAgent} developers \autocite{internAgent}, their system was able to outperform the \enquote{AI-Scientist-V2} \autocite{aiSci2}, a successor of \enquote{The AI Scientist} \autocite{aiScientist}.
	
	The work on the \enquote{InternAgent} \autocite{internAgent} also introduced tracking the cost of the AI usage during a run of the system. In this metric the \enquote{InternAgent} \autocite{internAgent} outperformed the \enquote{AI-Scientist-V2} \autocite{aiSci2} again, indicating that higher costs do not necessarily yield better results.

	A less automated approach was made by \citeauthor{sciSci} with the introduction of \enquote{SciSciGPT} \autocite{sciSci}. This system is developed for Science of Science research, which is why \enquote{SciSciGPT} is equipped with data management component amongst a literature review and an analytics component. Similar to the \enquote{InterAgent}, some form of orchestrator is used. It receives the outputs of the subtasks along with an evaluation from a task designated to critique the outputs of other tasks. In contrast to the \enquote{AIScientist} \autocite{aiScientist} and \enquote{InternAgent} \autocite{internAgent}, \enquote{SciSciGPT} \autocite{sciSci} does not generate its own research ideas. Instead it responds to an input prompt, allowing for seamless integration into the work of human scientists.
	
	As of \DTMDate{2026-06-01}, the latest advancement in the field was made by \citeauthor{pIntern} presenting the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern}, \enquote{a multi-agent scaffolding system that takes a physics problem and works through it autonomously} \autocite[section 3]{pIntern}. Their approach is similar to previous systems, using an orchestrator to manage a research and computation component. The results are reviewed and checked against the literature, before the critique is passed to the \enquote{Strategy Planner} \autocite{pIntern}, which has access to a literature \enquote{Background Survey} \autocite{pIntern} and can adjust the orchestration strategy. This process is repeated until the review deems the result correct or the maximum number of iterations is reached. The \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern} was able to exceed the performance of the models used for its \glspl{Agent} by over \si{75 \percent}, highlighting the potential of orchestration frameworks \autocite{pIntern}.
	
	This Thesis aims to develop a system with functionality similar to the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern} (at the time the work on this Thesis began, the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern} was not yet published). The Physics Modelling Assistant receives a physics problem or modelling request and crafts an according model along with its implementation in order to answer the request.
	The performance of the system is measured to provide inside into its usefulness. In contrast to the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern}, the Physics Modelling Assistant uses comparably weaker models, aiming to explore if improvements similar to the \enquote{physics-intern}'s \autocite{pIntern} can be achieved. The influence of some of the system's configurations is explored by measuring their effect on the performance, the focus is on the effect of external information. With this work I want to contribute to a better understanding of \gls{Agentic AI}'s parameters and a more efficient use of \gls{AI} systems in scientific research.

	\section{Theoretical Foundations}
	Before going in to the details of \gls{AI}-based systems, some key terms are be defined.
	
	\subsection{Definitions}
	
	\subsubsection{Artificial Intelligence} \label{subsub:ai}
	Artificial Intelligence (\gls{AI}) describes the functionality of a system. 
	There is no universally accepted definition for \gls{AI}, so for the purpose of this Thesis, the definition from \citeauthor{russell2023} \autocite[p.~22-26]{russell2023} is used. They describe \gls{AI} as being a rational actor (rational agent), which acts in a way that achieves the best result based on the expectation of what the result should be (\autocite[p,~26]{russell2023}, translated). This definition allows for a variety of systems to be considered an \gls{AI} and accounts for the uncertainty regarding the goal the system should work towards. Therefore, classical PID-controllers and software for processing and displaying data, as crafted by \citeauthor{diplom} \autocite{diplom}, could be considered \gls{AI} systems.
	
	\subsubsection{Large Language Model} \label{subsub:llm}
	At its core a Language Model is a \enquote{Transformer Language Model} \autocite{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels} as described by \citeauthor{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels}. It operates on small words or parts of words, which are called \glspl{token}.
	
	When a \gls{LLM} receives an input string, it divides it into \glspl{token} and puts into the transformation function. Based on these input \glspl{token} and the previous output \glspl{token}, the model calculates the most likely next output \gls{token}. The number of \glspl{token} a model can see, and therefore consider, is limited, the limit is called the \gls{context-window} of the model \autocite{howLLMwork}. The final result of the \gls{LLM} is its calculation for most likely sequence of output \glspl{token} given the provided input, which means, it provides the best result for the given goal, thereby fulfilling the definition of \gls{AI} (see \autoref{subsub:ai}). The process of developing capable \gls{LLM}s will not be discussed in this Bachelor's Thesis.
	
	The distinction between Language Models and Large Language Models is in the number of internal parameters a model has. As stated in \citetitle{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels} \autocite{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels}, there is no hard cut-off for a Language Models to be considered Large. Commonly, models which are considered Large Language Models have $\geq 10 \cdot 10^{9}$ parameters.
	
	\subsubsection{Agentic \gls{AI}} \label{subsub:Agentic}
	As described by \citeauthor{agenticAiDefinitions}, Agentic \gls{AI}s are \enquote{autonomous, goal-driven systems that can operate on their own for long periods, requiring minimal human supervision} \autocite[section:3. Results]{agenticAiDefinitions}. In order to achieve this behaviour, these systems combine abilities which are beyond \gls{LLM}s. \citeauthor{agenticAiDefinitions} highlight the \enquote{strategic planning} \autocite[section:3. Results]{agenticAiDefinitions} capabilities, the preservation of the tasks context over time \autocite{agenticAiDefinitions}, the usage of \enquote{external tools to extend [the systems] capabilities} \autocite{agenticAiDefinitions} and the collaboration of individual Agents (this means \gls{AI}-systems) with other Agents. \autocite{agenticAiDefinitions}
	
	\subsection{Decoding the Output of \gls{LLM}s}
	In \autoref{subsub:llm}, \gls{LLM} is defined based on the functionality it provides. In order to understand how the behaviour of \gls{LLM}s can be modified, a more formal definition is given, based on the work of \citeauthor{llmDetail} in \citetitle{llmDetail} \autocite{llmDetail}.
	
	\subsubsection{\gls{LLM} as a Function which Assigns Probabilities} \label{subsub:contextWindow}
	A \gls{LLM} can be described as a function $f$ with internal parameters $\theta$. Additionally, $f$ takes a set of input \gls{token} $v_{in} = (i_1, ..., i_N)$, where $N \in \mathbb{N}$ is the number of input \glspl{token}, and a set of previous output \glspl{token} $v_{out,prev} = (o_{-1}, ..., o_{-M})$, where $M \in \mathbb{N}$ is the number of previous output \glspl{token} considered. The function $f(\theta, v_{in}, v_{outp,prev})$ assigns a probability $s(v)$ to every \gls{token} $v \in \mathcal{V}$, where $\mathcal{V}$ is the vocabulary of \glspl{token} known to the \gls{LLM}:
	\begin{equation}
		f(\theta, v_{in}, v_{outp,prev}) = \begin{pmatrix}
			s(v'_1) \\
			... \\
			s(v'_Z)
		\end{pmatrix} = s(v) \ \ \ \ \text{where $Z$ is the size of the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$} \label{LLM as function}
	\end{equation}
	$v'_i$ with $i \in [1,Z] \subset \mathbb{N}$ is an element from the ordered set of $\mathcal{V}'$, which allows to map the rows of the probability matrix $s(v)$ to the \glspl{token} of the vocabulary.
	The probability indicates how well a \gls{token} is suited to be the next output \gls{token} \autocite[p.~4]{llmDetail}. The probabilities $s(v_i')$ are called \enquote{\glspl{logit}} \autocite[p.~4]{llmDetail}, they are not normalized by default and larger values of a \gls{logit} signal a better fit for the corresponding \gls{token} to be the next output \gls{token}.
	
	The \gls{LLM} by itself outputs a probability distribution including every \gls{token} from its vocabulary. To choose a \gls{token} based on this probability distribution, \citeauthor{llmDetail} state that the following optimization problem needs to be solved, they label it the \enquote{MASTER PROBLEM} \autocite[p.~5]{llmDetail}:
	\begin{equation}
		q^*= \mathrm{arg}\max_{q \in \Delta(\mathcal{V})} \left[\underbrace{\langle q, s \rangle}_{\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} q(v) \cdot s(v)} - \lambda \Omega(q)\right] \label{masterProblem}
	\end{equation}
	The used quantities are:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $q^*$ is the probability distribution maximizing \autoref{masterProblem}
		\item $\Delta (\mathcal{V})$ is the set of all possible probability distributions
		\item $q$ is a single probability distribution for a given vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$
		\item $s$ is the initial vector of \glspl{logit} the \gls{LLM} produces
		\item $\Omega(q)$ is a regularisation function, which can push $q^*$ towards a certain distribution
		\item $\lambda$ is the strength of the regularisation function, with $\lambda \geq 0$
	\end{itemize}
	There are different approaches for finding $q^*$ and eventually getting a single \gls{token} from it, the once relevant to this Thesis are discussed in the following.
	
	\subsubsection{Greedy Decoding} \label{subsub:greedy}
	This method uses no special regularisation function $\Omega(q)$, and therefore sets $\lambda=0$. This simplifies \autoref{masterProblem} to:
	\begin{equation}
		q^* = \mathrm{arg}\max_{q \in \Delta (\mathcal{V})}\langle q,s\rangle \label{eq:greedyDec}
	\end{equation}
	The resulting $q^*$ vector has all the probability mass placed on the \gls{token}, which was calculated to be most likely, while all other \glspl{token} get assigned a probability of 0. Therefore, the vector $q^*$ has value 0 in every row except the one corresponding to the most likely \gls{token}, which has probability 1. This means the next \gls{token} is always the most likely \gls{token} according to the \gls{LLM}'s calculations. If multiple \glspl{token} have the highest probability $s(v)$, a single \gls{token} is selected amongst them. \autocite[p.~10]{llmDetail}.
	
	\subsubsection{Softmax Decoding} \label{subsub:softmax}
	For Softmax Decoding, the regularisation function is chosen as $\Omega(q) = - \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} q(v) \log(q(v))$, which is the negative entropy of $q$. Using this $\Omega(q)$ in solving \autoref{masterProblem} leads to the following optimal distribution $q^*$, where $q_t^*(v)$ is the probability per \gls{token}: \autocite[p.~10-12]{llmDetail}
	\begin{equation}
		q_t^*(v) = \frac{\exp(\frac{s(v)}{\lambda})}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{V}}\exp(\frac{s(u)}{\lambda})}
	\end{equation}
	This distribution is normalised and has the parameter $\lambda$, which controls the spread of the probability distribution $q^*$. For larger values of $\lambda$, the probability is spread more evenly across all \glspl{token}, which results in previously unlikely \glspl{token} being more probable than before. When $\lambda$ becomes very small, the probability distribution is sharper, with the probability mass being concentrated more around a few \glspl{token} which already where likely before. The next output \gls{token} is selected according to the final probability distribution.
	For the edge case $\lambda = 0$, the regularisation function is disabled (see \autoref{masterProblem}), which causes the Softmax Decoding to become Greedy Decoding (see \autoref{subsub:greedy}). \autocite[p.~10-12]{llmDetail}
	
	The parameter $\lambda$ is one way of controlling the general randomness of the output. In the context of \gls{LLM}s, this parameter is often associated with the creativity of a model and called \textbf{Temperature} $T$. \autocite[p.~10-12]{llmDetail}
	
	\subsubsection{Top-k Decoding} \label{subsub:topk}
	Top-k Decoding extends Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) by selecting the $k$ ($k \in \mathbb{N} \setminus k = 0$) \glspl{token} with the highest \gls{logit} values \autocite[p.~12]{llmDetail}. These \glspl{token} form a subset of the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ called $\mathcal{V}_k$ on which Softmax Decoding is performed, while the probability for every other \gls{token} $v \in \mathcal{V} \setminus \mathcal{V}_k$ is set to $0$.
	This gives the following probability distribution \autocite[p.~12]{llmDetail}:
	\begin{equation}
		q_t^*(v) = \begin{cases}
			\frac{e^{\frac{s(v)}{\lambda}}}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{V}_k}e^{\frac{s(u)}{\lambda}}} & \text{ for } v \in \mathcal{V}_k\\
			0 & \text{ else }\\
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
	For $k \geq \lvert\mathcal{V}\rvert$, Top-k Decoding is equivalent to the standard Softmax Decoding (see \autoref{subsub:softmax}). Setting $k = 1$ results in the same functionality as Greedy Decoding (see \autoref{subsub:greedy}). \autocite[p.~12]{llmDetail}
	
	From Top-k Decoding the parameter \textbf{top\_k} is derived, which can be used to shape the output of \gls{LLM}s \autocite[p.~12]{llmDetail}.
	
	\subsubsection{Top-p Decoding} \label{subsub:topp}
	Top-p Decoding is another extension to Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}), also selecting a subset of all \glspl{token} called $\mathcal{V}_p$ from the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. In contrast to Top-k Decoding (\autoref{subsub:topk}), Top-p Decoding defines a certain amount of probability $p \in \left(\right.0,1\left.\right]$, on which Softmax Decoding is performed. This is achieved by selecting the \gls{token} with the highest probability $s(v)$ from the set $\mathcal{V} \setminus \mathcal{V}_p$ and adding it to the set $\mathcal{V}_p$, thereby removing it from $\mathcal{V} \setminus \mathcal{V}_p$. This is repeated until the sum of the probabilities of all \glspl{token} $v \in \mathcal{V}_p$ exceeds the value of $p$. The probability of the \glspl{token} in $\mathcal{V}\setminus \mathcal{V}_p$ is set to $0$, while Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) is performed on the \glspl{token} in $\mathcal{V}_p$. The resulting probability distribution is (\autocite{llmDetail}):
	\begin{equation}
		q_t^*(v) = \begin{cases}
			\frac{e^{\frac{s(v)}{\lambda}}}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{V}_p}e^{\frac{s(u)}{\lambda}}} & \text{ for } v \in \mathcal{V}_p\\
			0 & \text{ else}\\
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
	For $p=1$, Top-p Decoding is equivalent to Softmax Decoding, since all \glspl{token} are included in $\mathcal{V}_p$. For $p\rightarrow 0$, only the \gls{token} with the highest probability gets a non $0$ probability, which makes this case equivalent to Greedy Decoding (see \autoref{subsub:greedy}). \autocite{llmDetail}
	
	Top-p Decoding allows for dynamic adaptation based on how sharp or spread out the probability distribution returned by the \gls{LLM} is. For a sharp probability distribution, only the few very likely \glspl{token} keep their calculated probabilities and Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) is performed on them. Whereas, for a flatter distribution, a higher number of \glspl{token} are included into the Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) and therefore are possible output \glspl{token}. \autocite{llmDetail}
	
	The derived parameter is called \textbf{top\_p} and is commonly used in the field of \gls{LLM}s to modify a system's output \autocite[p.~13]{llmDetail}.
	
	\subsection{Other Parameters which Can Influence an \gls{AI} System's Performance}
	As shown by numerous benchmarks (\autocite{huggArena}, \autocite{faceLeaderBoard}), different \gls{LLM}s achieve different performances. Some of the important factors for these differences are discussed in the following.
	\subsubsection{Selection of the \gls{LLM}}
	One of the most noticeable differences in \gls{LLM}s is the number of internal parameters they use. The general assumption is that more parameters lead to better performance. Another important feature is the amount of data used to train a \gls{LLM}. As shown by \citeauthor{powerLaw} \autocite[p.~5]{powerLaw}, combining the number of internal parameters $N$ a model uses, and the number of \glspl{token} $D$ used in training a model can predict the \enquote{cross-entropy loss} \autocite[p.~6]{powerLaw} $L$ of a \gls{LLM}:
	\begin{equation}
		L(N,D) = \left[\left(\frac{N_C}{N}\right)^{\frac{\alpha_N}{\alpha_D}} + \frac{D_C}{D}\right]^{\alpha_D} \label{powerLawEq}
	\end{equation}
	The used quantities are:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $L(N,D)$ is the \enquote{cross-entropy loss} \autocite[p.~6]{powerLaw}. Lower Values indicate a better model.
		\item $N_C$ is the critical number of internal parameters, until which \autoref{powerLawEq} is applicable. $N_C \approx 8.8 \cdot 10^{13}$ \autocite{powerLaw}
		\item $N$ is the number of internal parameters the model uses.
		\item $D_C$ is the critical number of \glspl{token}, until which \autoref{powerLawEq} is applicable. $D_C \approx 5.4 \cdot 10^{13}$ \autocite{powerLaw}
		\item $\alpha_N$ is the exponent for predicting $L(N)$. $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$ \autocite{powerLaw}
		\item $\alpha_D$ is the exponent for predicting $L(D)$. $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$ \autocite{powerLaw}
	\end{itemize}
	
	There are also other design and training related influences on a models performance, \citeauthor{powerLaw} discus the relevance of the number of training steps or the batch size (\glspl{token} per step) \autocite{powerLaw}. \citeauthor{modelSelection} \autocite{modelSelection} show the importance of how the training data are composed, highlighting the increases in predicting a models performance when considering this factor.
	
	\subsubsection{Tool Use of \gls{LLM}s} \label{subsub:tool}
	Since all \gls{LLM}s have a limited amount of training data, for every model there are domains it lacks basic knowledge in, hindering it to perform tasks in this domain effectively. In order to mitigate this, \gls{LLM}s are fed extra information to help them answer the question at hand. Realizing this via human input is inefficient and impractical, especially when the human itself is not very knowledgeable in the domain at hand. For these reasons, \gls{LLM}s are getting equipped with tools, allowing them to access information, which is not included in their training data. As shown by \citeauthor{websearch1} \autocite{websearch1} and \citeauthor{websearch2} \autocite{websearch2}, equipping a \gls{LLM} with some sort of web search tool allows to reduces the false outputs a system produces. Note that tool use is one of the features making an \gls{AI} an \gls{Agentic AI}, as discussed in \autoref{subsub:Agentic}.
	
	Another common class of tools are file readers, they can be able to parse various formats like PDF, JSON, or plain text.
	
	Code execution tools allow a \gls{LLM} to run generated code autonomously. This introduces the problem of untrusted code execution, since the model could generate malicious code, accessing private information or modifying important data on the system it executes the code on. Executing untrusted code should therefore be done in a container separate from important data and systems. As stated by \citeauthor{sandboxEscape} \autocite{sandboxEscape}, these containers, called Sandboxes, are not always fully secure; strong \gls{LLM}s are able to escape them sometimes.

	\subsubsection{Reasoning} \label{subsub:reasoning}
	In the context of \gls{LLM}s, reasoning is creating a chain-of-thought, which means partitioning one big task into several smaller subtasks. A chain-of-thought improves the performance of \gls{LLM}s on tasks which require a multi-step process to obtain the correct solution, as shown by \citeauthor{reasoningGood} \autocite{reasoningGood}. 
	
	\subsection{Orchestrating \gls{AI}s to Behave as an \gls{Agentic AI}}
	As mentioned in \autoref{subsub:Agentic}, combining multiple \gls{AI} systems into one enables the resulting system to perform more complex tasks and achieve better results. The individual \gls{AI} systems of an \gls{Agentic AI} system are commonly referred to as \glspl{Agent}. In the following, the main approaches on how \glspl{Agent} can work together are presented, as there where outlined by \citeauthor{orchStyles} \autocite{orchStyles}.
	
	\subsubsection{Orchestration Architectures}
	\paragraph{The Sequential Pipeline} \autocite[p.~3]{orchStyles}\label{par:pipe} is a linear chain, where each \gls{Agent} is executed one after another \autocite{orchStyles}. Individual \glspl{Agent} or their tools can be rerun, as long as the pipeline is still in the corresponding step. But once an \gls{Agent} is done, it can not be called again (in the same run). The design process is relatively simple and the resulting system show very deterministic behaviour, which allows errors to be located easily. \autocite{orchStyles}
	
	\paragraph{The Parallel Fan-Out with Merge} \autocite[p.~4]{orchStyles} \label{par:paral} differs from the Sequential Pipeline (\autoref{par:pipe}), by allowing certain \glspl{Agent} to run simultaneously. After the parallel \glspl{Agent} are done with their tasks, their results are merged by the next sequential \gls{Agent}. Introducing an \gls{Agent} before the parallelization allows for each parallel \gls{Agent} to only receive the information relevant to its task. Therefore, each parallel \gls{Agent} has to deal with less noise and receives less input \glspl{token}, which helps to stay within the \gls{LLM}'s \gls{context-window}. The \gls{Agent} responsible for merging resolves conflicts like contradicting information of parallel \glspl{Agent}. The parallel design allows for the highest throughput compared to the other architectures \autocite[p.~7]{orchStyles}.
	
	\paragraph{The Hierarchical Supervisor-Worker} \autocite[p.~4-5]{orchStyles} \label{par:hier} architecture introduces a supervisor \gls{Agent}. It dynamically decides which \glspl{Agent} (Subagents) to call and which tasks the Subagents should handle. Furthermore, the supervisor \gls{Agent} reviews the Subagents' results and reruns the respective tasks, if it has low confidence in the result being correct. This forms a feedback loop, which allows the \gls{Agentic AI} system to dynamically assign more difficult tasks to more powerful \glspl{Agent}. The hierarchical architecture produces better results than the Sequential Pipeline (\autoref{par:pipe}) and the parallel design (\autoref{par:paral}), but is also more costly regarding the execution time. \autocite[p.~7]{orchStyles}
	
	\paragraph{The Reflexive Self-Correcting Loop} \autocite[p.~5]{orchStyles} \label{par:reflex} executes the \glspl{Agent} in a predefined order similar to the Sequential Pipeline (\autoref{par:pipe}), but adds a verification step. The verification is performed by an \gls{Agent}, which either deems the result to be correct (enough) and declares it to be the system's output, or sends it back for correction. Before the result is reintroduced into the system, it is critiqued by stating the problems encountered, which allows for targeted corrections on the next cycle. This architecture refines the output over time rather than simply rerunning the entire system. The Reflexive Self-Correction Loop \autocite[p.~5]{orchStyles} achieves the highest accuracy but also comes at the highest cost of all architectures discussed. \autocite[p.~7]{orchStyles}
	
	When interpreting the performances of the architectures discussed, it should be taken into account that \citeauthor{orchStyles} \autocite[p.~14-15]{orchStyles} tested them on their extraction capabilities from financial documents.
	Therefore, the performances could change in different domains and for different tasks. When designing \gls{Agentic AI} systems, the combination of multiple architectures is possible.
	
	\subsubsection{Communication Inside \gls{Agentic AI} Systems} \label{subsub:protocols}
	Communication protocols are used to define how the different components of an \gls{Agentic AI} system interact. An orchestration framework may implement some or all of these protocols or similar ones. The \gls{LLM}s are wrapped into a layered architecture, which handles the interactions between different \gls{LLM}s and manages how tools are called. \autocite{howToolsAreUsed}
	
	\paragraph{The Model Context Protocol (MCP)} \autocite{orchFrameworks} (documentation: \autocite{MCP}) handles how the underlying \gls{LLM} of an \gls{Agent} uses tools.
	
	\paragraph{The Agent Communication Protocol (ACP)} \autocite{orchFrameworks} (documentation: \autocite{ACP}) allows different \glspl{Agent} to communicate with each other. It is based on a client-server architecture and uses \enquote{RESTful} communication \autocite{protocols}.
	
	\paragraph{The Agent-to-Agent Protocol (A2A)} \autocite{orchFrameworks} (documentation: \autocite{A2A}) handles \gls{Agent} interactions to enable effective cooperation. This is achieved by \glspl{Agent} communicating their capabilities to each other \autocite{protocols}.
	
	\paragraph{The Agent Network Protocol (ANP)} \autocite{orchFrameworks} (documentation: \autocite{ANP}) manages decentralised communication between \glspl{Agent}. It allows for secure interactions over the internet \autocite{protocols}.
	
	\subsubsection{Memory} \label{subsub:memory}
	In the context of \gls{Agentic AI}, memory is an \gls{Agent}'s awareness of previous actions. Short-term memory is the context of the task the \gls{Agent} is currently performing. Long-term memory allows an \gls{Agent} to access information it obtained during previous runs \autocite{orchFrameworks}. Other kinds of memory include \enquote{semantic memory} \autocite{orchFrameworks}, \enquote{procedural memory} \autocite{orchFrameworks}, and \enquote{episodic memory} \autocite{orchFrameworks}, but these will not be discussed in this Thesis.
	
	\subsection{Evaluation of \gls{AI} Systems} \label{sub:eval}
	In order to measure the influence of an \gls{AI} system's configurations, a metric for the systems performance is required. One approach is testing the \gls{AI} system on a set of multiple choice questions, with each answer being clearly right or wrong \autocite{mmlu}. The total score over the set of questions indicates the system's performance. This method is used for the \enquote{GPQA Diamond} benchmark \autocite{gpqaDiamondPaper} and partly for the \enquote{ Massive Multi-discipline Multimodal Understanding and Reasoning (MMMU)} \autocite{MMMU} benchmark, which are both part of the \citeauthor{artAnaLeaderboard} \citetitle{artAnaLeaderboard} \autocite{artAnaLeaderboard}.
	
	Another option are open answer questions, which require a written response by the tested model. This method is used by \enquote{SciBench} \autocite{scibench}, where the answers usually are short and need to match the expected answer exactly, since a string comparison of them is performed. The performance over all questions is accumulated and provides a final evaluation score of the tested system.
	
	In order to properly assess an \gls{AI} system's capability to handle more complex tasks, human grading can be employed. This is done by Arena Intelligence \autocite{arena}, where users write a prompt and pick the best response from two anonymous answers. Human grading is also used by in \enquote{SciEx} \autocite{humanGrading}, where experts evaluate the \gls{LLM}'s results. As acknowledged by \citeauthor{humanGrading} \autocite{humanGrading}, human grading is not feasible for large-scale benchmarking due to its time and resource inefficiency.
	
	\subsubsection{CritPt} \label{subsub:critPt}
	Presented by \citeauthor{critPtArx}, \enquote{CritPt} \autocite{critPtArx} introduces checkpoints which allow to identify a model’s progress on a question. This provides a more in‑depth view of the failure points of the evaluated system. The probed system is prompted with the question and generates an answer; afterwards, the required output format must be adopted to enable automatic evaluation. \citeauthor{critPtArx} describe using the checkpoints to guide the system towards the correct answer and also mention a mechanism which assists the tested system in adopting the required output format. \autocite{critPtArx}
	
	There are three types of questions, which require either a numerical value, a symbolic expression, or executable python code. Composite answers are only graded as correct if all individual parts are correct. \autocite{critPtArx}
	
	CritPt tests a system on 70 problems which are publicly available via their GitHub repository \autocite{critptGit}, the scores are averaged over five runs. The answers to the problem sets are not made public to prevent models from finding them, rendering the benchmark useless. Therefore, the models answers to the questions are submitted to an evaluation server, returning the final result of the benchmark. Users are limited to 10 submissions per 24 hours, in order to prevent leakage of the correct answers. For the same reason, not solutions or attempts of them should be publicised. \autocite[p.~10]{critPtArx}
	
	\section{Design of the Physics Modelling Assistant}
	In order to easily enable future work on and with the Physics Modelling Assistant, the choice was made to only use services which are open source and free of charge.
	
	\subsection{Orchestration Framework: CrewAI} \label{sub:crewAi}
	The decision was made not to design a new framework for orchestrating \gls{AI}s in order to save time on this software development heavy task and instead focus on the Physic Modelling Assistant's configurations. \citeauthor{orchFrameworks} \autocite{orchFrameworks} discuss a number of frameworks varying in complexity and beginner friendliness. For this Thesis, the CrewAI \autocite{crewAi} framework is used for its role focused style \autocite{orchFrameworks}, consisting of \glspl{Agent} and Tasks \autocite{crewAi} providing a level of abstraction from the technical details of orchestration. Every \gls{Agent} can be configured individually, which includes using different \gls{LLM}s for every \gls{Agent}. Every Task has a description and specifications regarding the output and the output format, along with the \gls{Agent} designated to it \autocite{taskPy}. On execution of a Task, its instructions are merged with the assigned \gls{Agent}'s, providing the input for the underlying \gls{LLM}. An \gls{Agent}'s behaviour is defined by its role, goal, and backstory. The role is a very short description comparable to a job title. The goal specifies the actions an \gls{Agent} is supposed to perform similar to a job description. Details for the \gls{Agent}'s behaviour are provided via the backstory argument, including preferences or advice on how to approach the Task. CrewAI allows to access any \gls{LLM}, provided the user presents a valid \gls{API key} for the model. The \gls{LLM} can be assigned parameters including the discussed Temperature (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) and top\_p (\autoref{subsub:topp}), the number of \glspl{token} to be used, and others. Tools can be assigned to both \glspl{Agent} and Tasks, with CrewAI providing ready to use tools, but also allowing to add custom tools \autocite{crewaiTool}.

	The organisation of multiple CrewAI \glspl{Agent} and Tasks is managed by the Crew \autocite{crewaiCrew}. As depicted in \autoref{fig:crewExe}, it is responsible for initializing and validating the \gls{Agent}'s and Task's configurations before launching the system. As stated by \citeauthor{orchFrameworks} \autocite{orchFrameworks}, CrewAI's orchestration combines elements from the ACP, A2A and ANP (see \autoref{subsub:protocols}). The CrewAI documentation \autocite{crewAi} and the source code available on GitHub \autocite{crewPy} show a sequential and a hierarchical execution approach. The sequential execution (see \autoref{fig:crewExe}) runs the Tasks in the order they are defined in the source code, thereby forming a sequential Pipeline (see \autoref{par:pipe}). This can be modified into a Parallel Fan-Out with Merge (see \autoref{par:paral}) by, allowing certain Tasks to run at the the same time, which CrewAI calls asynchronous \autocite{crewaiParallel}. Merging is handled by specific Task, waiting for all parallel Tasks to be done before starting its work (see Flows \autocite{crewaiFlow}). The hierarchical execution style (see \autoref{fig:crewExe}) of a CrewAI Crew enables a Hierarchical Supervisor-Worker architecture (see \autoref{par:hier}) by delegating Tasks, or aspects of them, to Subtasks. Implementing a Reflexive Self-Correcting Loop (see \autoref{par:reflex}) with CrewAI can be achieved by making use of Flows \autocite{crewaiFlow}, which allow for conditional calls and the coupling of multiple Crews.
	
	In order to achieve a deterministic workflow and allowing to easily make adjustments to the setup, the Sequential Pipeline architecture (\autoref{par:pipe}) is used for the Physics Modelling Assistant.

	\subsection{\gls{LLM}s Used}
	In order to use \gls{LLM}s effectively in an automated pipeline, a provider offering a program access to its \gls{LLM}s is needed. For this Thesis, the  \enquote{Scalable AI Accellarator (SAIA)} \autocite{saiaDoc} hosted by the \enquote{Gesellschaft für wissenschaftliche Datenverarbeitung mbH Göttingen} (GWDG) \autocite{gwdg} is used, offering limited but free access to a selection of open weight \gls{LLM}s. The models are accessed via an \gls{API key} issued by the GWDG. A list of the available \gls{LLM}s is provided on the SAIA webpage \autocite{saiaModels}, but can also be obtained via a request to the server hosting the models. To identify to most capable of the available models, individual benchmarks contributing to the \citetitle{artAnaLeaderboard} from \citeauthor{artAnaLeaderboard} are considered \autocite{artAnaLeaderboard}. The AA-LCR \autocite{aaAA} tests the long context reasoning, the GPQA Diamond \autocite{aaDiamond} tests for scientific reasoning capabilities, SciCode \autocite{aaSciCode} evaluates the models capabilities in science and coding, CritPt \autocite{aaCritPt} (see \autoref{subsub:critPt}) probes the physics based reasoning abilities of the \gls{LLM}s. The performances of all availabe models across the mentioned benchmarks are displayed in \autoref{saiaModelsBench}. They conclude qwen3.6 27B (27 billion parameters) to be the best model for long context reasoning, while qwen3.5 397B A17B (397 billion parameters in total, 17 billion active at a time) performs best in scientific reasoning tested by GPQA Diamond. glm-4.7 (358 billion parameters \autocite{glmParams}) achieves the highest score on the science and coding benchmark SciCode. For the CritPt benchmark both glm-4.7 and qwen3.5 397B A17B achieve the best results among the tested models, with both of them scoring at only \SI{1,7}{\percent}, indicating the difficulty of the CritPt benchmarking questions.
	
	Therefore, qwen3.6 27B is the \gls{LLM} of choice for all Tasks dealing with large inputs, while glm-4.7 is best suited for work requiring coding. When scientific reasoning is necessary, both qwen3.5 397B A17B and glm-4.7 are viable options.

	\subsection{Tools Used}
	To enhance the Physics Modelling Assistant's capabilities, certain \glspl{Agent} are equipped with tools (see \autoref{subsub:tool}). Each tool provides an object specifying the expected input at its constraints, both are visible to the \gls{Agent} using the tool.
	
	\subsubsection{arxiv\_paper\_tool with Exponential Backoff} \label{subsub:arxivPaperTool}
	CrewAI already provides the \enquote{arxiv\_Paper\_Tool} \autocite{crewaiArxivPaperTool}, allowing \glspl{Agent} to access the arXiv-API \autocite{arxiv} for finding papers relevant to the question at hand. During the development process of the Physics Modelling Assistant, the CrewAI tool's requests repeatedly kept getting denied, resulting in no papers to be found and downloaded from arXiv. As listed in the arXiv-API's Terms of Use, rate limits are enforced, blocking clients from sending many requests in a short amount of time. Furthermore, web servers deny repeated request from the same client for the same service \autocite{rechnernetze}. Since the CrewAI arxiv\_paper\_tool \autocite{crewaiArxivPaperTool} does not implement any strategy for waiting, its requests are eventually blocked by the arXiv-API servers. As a consequence an extending to the arxiv\_paper\_tool was made by implementing an exponential back-off strategy, as it is common for modern server requests \autocite{rechnernetze}. Additionally to the exponential back-off, the modified tool, called arxiv\_paper\_tool\_exponential\_backoff, adds a small random wait, which is common to prevent two clients requesting the same service from waiting for the same amount of timeblocking each others requests repeatedly \autocite{rechnernetze}. The tool finds a specified number of papers and downloads them to a designated directory.
	
	\subsubsection{pdf\_reader} \label{subsub:pdfReader}
	In order to access the papers downloaded from arXiv, a PDF reader tool was developed. It reads all PDF files from the specified directory, allowing the invoking \gls{Agent}'s \gls{LLM} to see the contents of the documents. In theory this allows to read any number and length of sources from the directory. In reality every \gls{LLM} has a \gls{context-window}, limiting the amount of \glspl{token} it can handle. When the sum of the \glspl{token} of the read documents exceeds the \gls{context-window}, the \gls{Agent} fails its Task. Among the SAIA models \autocite{saiaModels}, deepSeek V4 Flash offers the largest \gls{context-window}, supporting up to 1 million \glspl{token}, while qwen3.6 35B A3B provides the second‑largest \gls{context-window} at 262k \glspl{token}.
	
	Since the tool only allows reading files from a hard-coded subdirectory, it is deemed secure to be used outside of a sandbox environment (see \autoref{subsub:tool}).
	
	\subsubsection{dimensional\_analysis} \label{subsub:dimAna}
	During the testing of the system, formulas from source papers where stated correctly, but assigning correct units to the individual quantities proved to be difficult for the responsible \gls{Agent}. Mistakes in the unit assumptions lead to unphysical results or impossible units for the outcomes of the numerical implementation. To verify the consistency for all formulas used, the dimensional\_analysis tool was created. It is based on the symPy library and allows an \gls{Agent} to check the units it proposes. The tool takes an equation written as a string, a dictionary containing the used quantities and their proposed units, as well as a list of all units that are used. The units are substituted in for their respective quantities and both sides of the formula are simplified separately. Numerical values are not considered, since the aim is simply to achieve unit consistency. Lastly, the left side of the formula is divide by the right side, giving the unit correction needed on the right side for the formula to be consistent. Ideally, this value is 1, indicating no correction is needed. If it is not 1, the \gls{Agent} is supposed to interpret the unit mismatch correctly, by adjusting the assumed units for the used quantities. Another option for the \gls{Agent} is to modify the formula, which can be done by inserting physical constants. This allows to keep real world units while implementing a formula, which uses a different unit system.
	
	\subsubsection{Code Execution}
	As discussed by \citeauthor{sandboxEscape} \autocite{sandboxEscape}, a secure Sandbox is complex to design, but necessary, if \gls{LLM} generated code should be executed directly. As stated in \autoref{subsub:tool}, the weak points of Sandboxes can be exploited by capable \gls{LLM}s, which is why the Physics Modelling Assistant does not allow code execution.
	
	\subsection{Experimental Setup for the Pipeline} \label{sub:expSetup}
	The final system is designed to mirror a simplified research workflow consisting of finding relevant information, developing a model based on the knowledge acquired, and implementing the model to get an idea of what it predicts. In order to properly answers the questions from benchmarks (see \autoref{sub:eval}), the final Task is responsible for providing the output in the benchmark's required format.
	
	A simplified view of the Physics Modelling Assistant's steps is shown in \autoref{fig:tasks}, each step is performed by one Task and its corresponding \gls{Agent}.
	
	\subsubsection{Input} \label{subsub:input}
	The Physics Modelling Assistant receives the problem description, a string called \textbf{aim}, and instructions for the required format, also as a string. Additional parameters can be chosen via an interface, but defaults are set. The output directory specifies where the Tasks dump their output files; the directory's name consists of the question number and a unique identifier, ensuring previous outputs are not overwritten. The Temperature (see \autoref{subsub:softmax}) and the top\_p (see \autoref{subsub:topp}) parameters can be specified to shape the \gls{LLM}'s output. The \textbf{max\_tokens} parameter limits the number of \glspl{token} a \gls{LLM} produces. It is set to prevent the \gls{context-window} from being exceeded, which would cause the \gls{Agent} to fail its Task.
	
	Each \gls{Agent} can perform \textbf{reasoning} before it works on its Task. Reasoning implies that an \gls{Agent} creates a plan for how to approach the Task before working on it \autocite{crewaiAgentDoc} (see \autoref{subsub:reasoning}). The maximum number of reasoning tries can be specified; otherwise the \gls{Agent} retries to create a plan until an attempt is deemed sufficient \autocite{crewaiAgentDoc}. An \gls{Agent} can also rerun itself to improve its output if it deems this necessary. The maximum number of iterations is set through the \textbf{max\_iter} parameter \autocite{crewaiAgentDoc}, the default is 20. 
	
	The directory for the Tasks output files and the PDF downloads can be specified in the input as well.
	
	\subsubsection{General Parameter Choices} \label{subsub:paramCh}
	The Temperature and the top\_p parameter are the same for all \gls{Agent}, based on the recommendations from the GWDG webpage \autocite{saiaModels}. This aligns with the statements from \autoref{subsub:contextWindow}, describing the decoding of a \gls{LLM}'s output as an optimization problem. For glm-4.7 and qwen3.6 27B the recommended parameters are: \textbf{Temperature} = 1.0 and \textbf{top\_p} = 0.95. For qwen3.5 397B the recommendation is: \textbf{Temperature} = 0.6 and \textbf{top\_p} = 0.95. For the reasons discussed in \autoref{subsub:buildModel}, the qwen3.5 397B model is not used, which allows to set the Temperature and top\_p globally.
	
	The maximum number of output \glspl{token} is set to \si{50 \kilo} \autocite{crewaiAgentDoc} to ensure the \gls{context-window} is not exceeded. This is especially relevant for the \gls{Agent} extracting information from the sources.
	
	Each \gls{Agent} is allowed up to three iterations (\textbf{max\_iter} = 3) to refine its result if it deems this necessary \autocite{crewaiAgentDoc}. Reiterations allows a single \gls{Agent}'s result to be improved, if its output is of bad quality. This can happen due to the complexity of the Task, the inherent randomness of \gls{LLM}s, or temporary problems with accessing servers. Furthermore, retries can mitigate the influence of infrequent failures like the \gls{context-window} being exceeded.
	
	In order to balance the performance gains from reasoning (\autoref{subsub:reasoning}) with the need to limit excessive retries (\autoref{subsub:input}), the \textbf{max\_reasoning\_attempts} parameter is set to 3 globally.
	
	The Physics Modelling Agent does not use specific memory (\autoref{subsub:memory}) but relies on CrewAI passing the outputs of previous \glspl{Agent} to later ones via a shared output object (see \autocite[line 1610]{crewPy}).
	
	\subsubsection{Obtaining Information}
	\paragraph{Source Gathering}
	In order to get sufficient information for building a model, the arXiv-API \autocite{arxiv} is accessed via the modified arxiv\_paper\_tool (see \autoref{subsub:arxivPaperTool}) and the found papers are downloaded. The corresponding \textbf{source gatherer} \gls{Agent} is based on the qwen3.6 27B \gls{LLM} and is tasked to use its tool for finding papers related to the aim. The \textbf{gathering task} specifies that three to six papers should be found, more papers bare the risk of exceeding the \gls{context-window} of the used \gls{LLM}s. The Task further specifies which information should be listed for each source: arXiv ID, author, data, URL and a short summary. This content is written into a markdown report, allowing a human to inspect the sources found.
	
	This \gls{Agent} does not use reasoning because its Task is deemed to not need a multi step approach. All other parameters are kept at the global settings discussed in \autoref{subsub:paramCh}.
	
	Because of the issues with the arXiv-API rate limits \autocite{arxiv}, this step is packaged into its own Crew, allowing it to be executed independently from all later steps. This way changes or reruns intended to result in different behaviour of later steps can be made without rerunning the arXiv request again. For the measurements of the Physics Modelling Assistants performance in this Thesis, the source gathering is run again on every retry.
	
	\paragraph{Information Extraction} \label{par:infoExtr}
	The source finding Task saves its papers to the specified output directory. By knowing the directory's location, the \textbf{information extractor} \gls{Agent} can use its pdf\_reader (see \autoref{subsub:pdfReader}) to read the content of the files in that directory. The qwen3.6 27B \gls{LLM} is used for its superior long context reasoning score (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}). Its \gls{context-window} of \si{262 \kilo} \glspl{token} is the second largest only to deepSeek V4 Flash's \si{1 \mega} \autocite{saiaDoc} \autocite{saiaModels}. Since deepSeek V4 Flash achieves less than half of qwen3.6 27B's score in long context reasoning \autoref{saiaModelsBench}, the latter is used.
	
	The \textbf{information extractor} is instructed to find the information needed to build a model that accomplishes what the aim asks for. It should present its findings with scientific citation and its backstory emphasises that the \gls{Agent} should stay true to the source material it has available. The corresponding \textbf{extraction task} specifies that the output should be in markdown format, enforcing an unambiguous and human readable output for later \glspl{Agent} and inspecting the Tasks markdown output file.
	
	The parameters are kept at the global settings as described in \autoref{subsub:paramCh}
	
	\subsubsection{Building the Model} \label{subsub:buildModel}
	\paragraph{Modelling} \label{par:Modelling}
	For building the model which is at the heart of the Physics Modelling Assistant, the \gls{LLM} strongest in scientific and specifically physics reasoning should be used. Therefore, the first choice was qwen3.5 397B A17B performing the best at scientific reasoning (GPQA Diamond \autocite{gpqaDiamondPaper}) and joined best at CritPt \autocite{critPtArx}, as shown in \autoref{saiaModelsBench}. During the testing phase, requests to this model kept failing with an answer not being provided by the server before a timeout was reached. For that reason the glm-4.7 \gls{LLM} is chosen instead, for it being the joined second best model at scientific reasoning (GPQA Diamond \autocite{gpqaDiamondPaper}) and joined best model at CritPt \autocite{critPtArx} (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}). The \textbf{modeller} \gls{Agent} is prompted to provide a mathematical description for a model which accomplishes the aim. It is further instructed to rely on the information provided by the previous Task (extraction Task), provide a proper explanation for the steps taken, and not to write any code. The last instruction targets the \gls{Agent}'s previous behaviour of occasionally writing code which already implements the model. This is not wanted due to potential modifications needed for the model to be sufficient.
	
	The \textbf{modelling task} defines the expected output to be a complete mathematical description with a textual explanation. The requested format is once again markdown ensuring human readability of the generated output file. The default parameters setting described in \autoref{subsub:paramCh} are adopted.
	
	\paragraph{Unit Check}
	The \textbf{unit checker} \gls{Agent} accounts for custom parameter definitions and other conventions preventing a straight forward adoption of the model and its formulas. It uses the glm-4.7 \gls{LLM} and its goal specifies which steps to take. In the beginning the units of all the quantities should be determined and based on these assumptions, the dimensional\_analysis tool (see \autoref{subsub:dimAna}) is used to identify potential inconsistencies in the units assumed. Based on the tool's results, the \gls{Agent} is expected to correct its unit assumptions or formulas if deemed necessary.
	
	The \textbf{unit checking task} specifies that the quantities of the units and the output of the dimensional analysis tool should be displayed in the markdown output file. The parameter configuration follows the default (see \autoref{subsub:paramCh}).
	
	\paragraph{Parameters Suggestion}
	In order to obtain a meaningful simulation, physically realistic starting conditions are required. They are provided by the \textbf{parameter suggester}, which has the goal of finding typical starting parameters for the model simulation, and stating how these suggestions where derived. The \textbf{parameter suggestion task} highlights that explanations for the choice of parameters should be included in the markdown output file. The input parameters are unchanged compared to the default and the glm-4.7 model is used for the reasons discussed in \autoref{par:Modelling}.
	
	\subsubsection{Implementing the Model}
	\paragraph{Physics Simulation} \label{par:physicSim}
	In order to capture the suggested model accurately, the \textbf{simulation physician} \gls{Agent} is run with the goal of implementing the formulas of the model precisely, without making changes to them. The \gls{Agent} is based on the glm-4.7 \gls{LLM}, since it performs the best in coding and science among the available models (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}). As specified by the \textbf{physician simulation task}, the implementation is supposed to use the units determined by the unit checking task. To get human readable and interpretable results, the task is requested to create graphics if it deems this appropriate. The output is a Python file which should only contain executable Python code, all explanations should be in comments. The parameters are kept the same as before.
	
	\paragraph{Simulation Correction}
	According to its specifications the physics simulation task is meant to capture the models logic into working code, it is not asked to prioritize producing error free code. The correction of bugs is done by the \textbf{simulation correcter}, which is told not change the implemented formulas, but to correct coding mistakes and improve the codes efficiency and style. The corresponding \textbf{simulation correction task} defines the expected output to be working python code. The parameter configurations and \gls{LLM} are unchanged compared to the simulation physician in \autoref{par:physicSim}.
	
	\subsubsection{Giving a Final Answer}
	Benchmarks like CritPt expect a comprehensive answer (see \autoref{sub:eval}) to their questions, for that reason the \textbf{final outpter} \gls{Agent} is added to the pipeline. Its goal is to give a final answer and obey the format requested by the input question. Adapting the required format is crucial for CritPt's grading process \autocite{critPtArx}. The \textbf{final output task} specifies the output is supposed to be a string and produces a markdown output file. The glm-4.7 model is used for its performance on the CritPt benchmark seen \autoref{saiaModelsBench}, and the issues with qwen3.5 397B A17B discussed in \autoref{par:Modelling}.
	
	\subsubsection{Output} \label{subsub:outputBackticks}
	The output of the Physics Modelling Assistant is an object containing a string with the last Task's result as the answer to the benchmark'ss questions. Output files generated by each Task can be inspected by a human; the Python scripts are not directly executable since they contain triple backticks at the beginning and end. Presumably, this is done to mark the section in between as code, which is not accurate Python syntax. Removing or commenting the backticks results in the scripts becoming executable, apart from possible package installations.

	\subsubsection{Connecting to CritPt's Benchmark}
	CritPt is based on the inspec\_ai package, which allows to register a model by implementing the ModelAPI interface. This is achieved by the PMAModelAPI class, implementing the required generate method. This method receives the description of a CritPt challenge, but not its number or the required output format. This does not align with the statement form \autoref{subsub:critPt}, suggesting the system described by \citeauthor{critPtArx} \autocite{critPtArx} was modified compared to the one publicly available on GitHub \autocite{critptGit}. The same is true for CritPt guiding the system towards the correct result via checkpoints. The 70 publicly available challenges do not include any checkpoints and the GitHub repository does not mention a way to obtain them  autocite{critptGit}.
	
	The PMAModelAPI instance is programmed to identify the challenge based on the problem description it receives, obtaining the challenge number and the required output format. 
	
	Because of conflicting dependencies of the inspect\_ai and crewai packages reinquiring incompatible versions of the tiktoken and pydantic packages, the PMAModelAPI instance runs the Physics Modelling Assistant as a subprocess in a different virtual environment. It passes the challenge description and output format to the subprocess, along with the parameters the Physics Modelling Assistant exposes (see \autoref{subsub:paramCh}; Temperature, top\_p, max\_tokens, max\_iter, reasoning (True or False), max\_reasoning\_attempts). The challenge number along with a unique identifier and a file path are passed to the subprocess as well. This allows the processes to communicate via reading and writing to a file specified by the location and the identifier. This was implemented to work around the problem of extracting the Physics Modelling Assistant's output from the standard output of the subprocess.
	
	The subprocess consists of two layers. The top layer handles the communication with the super-process and starts the bottom layer, which is the Physics Modelling Assistant. The top layer handles the communication with the super-process. The Physics Modelling Assistant runs described in \autoref{sub:expSetup} and returns its final output to the top layer. There, the result is written into the file specified by the super-process, which waits for the subprocess to finish its execution, before attempting to read from the file.
	
	The generated results are stored in JSON-files and can be send to the evaluation server via a script provided by CritPt. Since Artificial Analysis \autocite{artifAna} has integrated CritPt, a valid \gls{API key} from Artificial Analysis \autocite{artifAna} is needed for the evaluation server. It can be obtained via Artificial Analysis and is free of charge. For the evaluation requests to be approved, the account connected to the submitting \gls{API key} needs to be marked as a trusted research account by Artificial Analysis \autocite{artAnaTrusted}.

		The \textbf{Rashba Hamiltonian} for a 2DEG at the $\Gamma$ point is [Bychkov \& Rashba, \textit{JETP Lett.} 39, 78 (1984)]:
		
		$$
		\boxed{\hat{H} = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \alpha\,\hat{z}\cdot(\mathbf{p}\times\boldsymbol{\sigma})}
		$$
		
		where:\begin{itemize}
		\item[-] $m$ is the effective carrier mass,
		\item[-] $\alpha$ is the Rashba spin–orbit coupling (SOC) strength,
		\item[-] $\mathbf{p} = \hbar\mathbf{k}$ is the momentum,
		\item[-] $\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z)$ are the Pauli matrices,
		\item[-] $\hat{z}$ is the direction perpendicular to the 2D plane.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{tcolorbox}
	Most contents are used from and attributed to the work of \citeauthor{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025} \autocite{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025}, these include the \textit{energy dispersion}, the \textit{spin eigenstates}, and the \textit{Fermi wavenumbers} split into high density regime (HDR) and low density regime (LDR). 	For the Magnetization, the following analytical results for the isotropic model are provided:
	\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, breakable, title=Analytical magnetization, colback=blue!5!white, colframe=blue!75!black] \label{pma:magn}
		\textbf{\Large4.1 Magnetization in the High-Density Regime (HDR)}
		
		For an electric field $\mathbf{E} = E_x\hat{x}$, the spin density along $\hat{y}$ in the HDR (both bands occupied) is [Gaiardoni et al., arXiv:2503.20712 (2025)]:
		
		$$
		\boxed{M_y = \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\,m\alpha\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y}
		$$
		
		\textbf{Key result:} In the HDR, the magnetization is \textbf{constant, independent of the Fermi energy} $E_F$, and proportional to the Rashba SOC parameter $\alpha$ and the effective mass $m$.
		
		\textbf{\Large4.2 Magnetization in the Low-Density Regime (LDR)}
		
		In the LDR (only the lower band occupied) [Gaiardoni et al., arXiv:2503.20712 (2025)]:
		
		$$
		\boxed{M_y = \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\,\sqrt{m^2\alpha^2 + 2mE_F}\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y}
		$$
		
		For small $E_F$ near the band crossing, this can be expanded as:
		
		$$
		M_y \approx \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\left(\alpha m + \frac{1}{2}\frac{E_F}{\alpha}\right)[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y
		$$
		
		The spin density \textbf{increases linearly with the Fermi energy}. In the limit $\alpha \to 0$, the second term vanishes because $E_F$ has a quadratic dependence on $\alpha$.
	\end{tcolorbox}
	Further information include the \textbf{Anisotropic Rashba Model}, the \textbf{Out-of-Plane Edelstein Effect}, the \textbf{Acoustic Edelstein Effect}, and the \textbf{Effect of a Magnetic Field}. The dependencies on the model's parameters is discussed with the following takeaways:

		\textbf{\Large Formula}
		$$
		\hat{H} = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \alpha\,\hat{z}\cdot(\mathbf{p}\times\boldsymbol{\sigma})
		$$
		
		\textbf{\large Quantities and Units}
		- $H$ (Hamiltonian): Energy $\rightarrow [E]$ (Joules)
		- $p$ (Momentum): $[p] = [M][L][T]^{-1}$
		- $m$ (Mass): $[m] = [M]$
		- $\alpha$ (Rashba parameter): $[\alpha] = ?$
		- $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ (Pauli matrices): Dimensionless
		
		\textbf{\large Tool Input}

			equation: H = p**2 / (2*m) + alpha * (p)
			dimensions: {"H": "energy", "p": "momentum", "m": "mass", "alpha": "alpha\_dimension"}
			unitList: energy, momentum, mass, alpha\_dimension
		\end{tcolorbox}

		\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!10!white]
			2*energy*mass/(momentum*(2*alpha\_dimension*mass + momentum))
		\end{tcolorbox}
		
		\textbf{\large Analysis}
		The first term $\frac{p^2}{2m}$ has units $\frac{([M][L][T]^{-1})^2}{[M]} = [M][L]^2[T]^{-2} = [E]$.
		The tool output shows that for consistency, \textit{alpha\_dimension} must satisfy \textit{energy = momentum * alpha\_dimension}.
		Since $[E] = [M][L]^2[T]^{-2}$ and $[p] = [M][L][T]^{-1}$, we must have:
		$$
		[\alpha] = \frac{[E]}{[p]} = \frac{[M][L]^2[T]^{-2}}{[M][L][T]^{-1}} = [L][T]^{-1}
		$$
		\textbf{Conclusion:} The Rashba parameter $\alpha$ has units of \textbf{velocity} ($m/s$)
	\end{tcolorbox}

		\textbf{Conclusion on Formula Consistency:}
		The formula is dimensionally consistent \textbf{if and only if} the system of units uses $\hbar=1$ (natural units) or the parameters $m$ and $\alpha$ are defined in units that include $\hbar$ (common in computational materials science, e.g., $\alpha$ in $eV\cdot\mathring{A}$ and $m$ in $eV^{-1}\mathring{A}^{-2}\cdot \hbar^2$).
		However, in standard SI units, the formula is missing a factor of $\hbar^{-2}$.
		
		\textbf{Correction for Standard Units:}
		$$
		M_y = \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi\hbar^2}\,m\alpha\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y
		$$
	\end{tcolorbox}

		\si{\ampere} &\overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\frac{\joule \meter^2}{\volt \second} \cdot \ampere \second \cdot \second \cdot \kilogram \cdot \frac{\meter}{\second} \cdot \frac{\volt}{\meter} \cdot \frac{1}{\joule^2 \second^2}} \notag\\

For further improvements architectural changes enabling feed back loops, and dynamically assigning Tasks to specialized \glspl{Agent} might be required. These changes would adapt common architectures from other \gls{Agentic AI} systems like the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern} or \enquote{SciSciGPT} \autocite{sciSci}, which use feedback loops as integral part of their systems. Within the CrewAI framework, feedback loops can be implemented via Flows \autocite{crewaiFlow}, utilizing required a significant expansion to the state of the Physics Modelling Assistant, with Flows expanding the Crew \autocite{crewPy} based design. Changes to the discussed setup should generally be considered, regarding the redundancy of the second simulation implementation step. Dynamically deciding which \glspl{Agent} to call should also improve the adaptability of the system regarding different types of input, with a dynamical setup able to omit specific \glspl{Agent} not needed to answer a concrete challenge or implement a broad model based on existing information.
	
	In its current state, the Physics Modelling Assistant is useful more as prototype than a helpful assistant for a human scientist. The design process and its result provide some insight into the design of \gls{Agentic AI} systems, pointing to the importance of a feedback architecture as the results from \citeauthor{orchStyles} \autocite{orchStyles} suggest. I believe the simple design is a good starting point for systematically investigating the influence of parameters on systems combining multiple \glspl{LLM}. By implementing a pipeline architecture it provides an interesting addition to the current landscape of \gls{Agentic AI} frameworks for science, allowing future work to compare the different architectures in more detail.
	
	Beyond the results presented so far, working on this project provided me with valuable insight into the field of \gls{AI} and especially \gls{Agentic AI}. This Bachelor's Thesis work has strengthened my interest to further explore the field of \gls{AI} systems.
