	\section{Introduction}
	Artificial Intelligence can be of great assistance in scientific research \autocite{aiHistWang}. As shown by \citeauthor{aiUseStat} \autocite{aiUseStat}, researchers in natural science are among the groups most familiar with \gls{AI} compared to other scientists. There is a wide range of applications, stretching from finding sources in the literature to aiding with the presentation of research findings. \gls{AI} can be useful for forming hypotheses and evaluating them by designing and controlling practical experiments and aiding during simulations, especially with the program implementation. \autocite{aiHistWang}
	
	In the past two years automated \gls{Agentic AI} research pipelines were developed with the potential to improve the work with \gls{AI} in science. The first fully autonomous approach was published by \citeauthor{aiScientist} in \citedate{aiScientist} \autocite{aiScientist}, introducing a framework called \enquote{The AI Scientist} \autocite{aiScientist}. It identifies interesting research questions, plans and executes according experiments, interprets the results, and writes a paper stating its findings via a multistep process. A similar framework is the \enquote{InternAgent} \autocite{internAgent}, which consists of the four distinct Tasks of \enquote{Survey}, \enquote{Code Review}, \enquote{Assessment}, and \enquote{Idea Innovation} \autocite{internAgent}. The interactions of these tasks are managed by the \enquote{Orchestration Agent} \autocite{internAgent}, which also allows for human feedback to be introduced into the system and handles how the findings of a workflow are communicated to the human scientist. This design shows some resemblance to human research groups with individuals specialized in specific aspects of the work while a supervisor coordinates the process. In tests conducted by the \enquote{InternAgent} developers \autocite{internAgent}, their system was able to outperform the \enquote{AI-Scientist-V2} \autocite{aiSci2}, a successor of \enquote{The AI Scientist} \autocite{aiScientist}.
	
	The work on the \enquote{InternAgent} \autocite{internAgent} also introduced tracking the cost of the AI usage during a run of the system. In this metric the \enquote{InternAgent} \autocite{internAgent} outperformed the \enquote{AI-Scientist-V2} \autocite{aiSci2} again, indicating that higher costs do not necessarily yield better results.

	A less automated approach was made by \citeauthor{sciSci} with the introduction of \enquote{SciSciGPT} \autocite{sciSci}. This system was developed for Science of Science research, which is why \enquote{SciSciGPT} is equipped with a data management component, a literature review component, and an analytics component. Similar to the \enquote{InterAgent}, an orchestrator is used, which receives the outputs of the subtasks along with an evaluation provided by a task designated to critique the outputs of other tasks. In contrast to the \enquote{AIScientist} \autocite{aiScientist} and \enquote{InternAgent} \autocite{internAgent}, \enquote{SciSciGPT} \autocite{sciSci} does not generate its own research ideas. Instead, it responds to an input prompt, allowing for seamless integration into the work of human scientists. \autocite{sciSci}
	
	As of \DTMDate{2026-06-01}, the latest advancement in the field of automated \gls{AI} system was made by \citeauthor{pIntern} presenting the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern}, \enquote{a multi-agent scaffolding system that takes a physics problem and works through it autonomously} \autocite[section 3]{pIntern}. Their approach is similar to previous systems, using an orchestrator to manage a research and computation component. The results are reviewed and checked against the literature, before the critique is passed to the \enquote{Strategy Planner} \autocite{pIntern}, which has access to a literature \enquote{Background Survey} \autocite{pIntern} and can adjust the orchestration strategy. This process is repeated until the review step deems the result correct or the maximum number of iterations is reached. The \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern} is able to exceed the performance of the underlying \gls{LLM}s used for its \glspl{Agent} by over \si{75 \percent}, highlighting the potential of orchestration frameworks \autocite{pIntern}.
	
	This Thesis aims to provide the foundations for an \gls{Agentic AI} system capable of assisting a human physicist in crafting models for their research work. The developed system, called \enquote{Physics Modelling Assistant}, can be tasked with a physics problem or modelling request and crafts an according model along with its implementation in order to answer the request.
	The performance of the system is measured on a state of the art benchmark to provide insight into its capabilities. In contrast to the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern}, the Physics Modelling Assistant mostly uses weaker \gls{LLM}s, aiming to explore if improvements similar to the \enquote{physics-intern}'s \autocite{pIntern} can be achieved. The influence of various system configurations on the performance is investigated, with focus on the provided external information. This work aims to contribute to a better understanding of the design principles underlying \gls{Agentic AI} and how such systems can be used more efficient in scientific research.

	\section{Theoretical Foundations}
	Before discussing the details of \gls{AI}-based systems, several key terms are introduced.
	
	\subsection{Definitions}
	
	\subsubsection{Artificial Intelligence} \label{subsub:ai}
	Artificial Intelligence (\gls{AI}) describes the functionality of a system. 
	There is no universally accepted definition for \gls{AI}, so for the purpose of this Thesis, the definition from \citeauthor{russell2023} \autocite[p.~22-26]{russell2023} is used. They describe \gls{AI} as being a \enquote{rational actor (rational agent), which acts in a way that achieves the best result based on the expectation of what the result should be} (\autocite[p.~26]{russell2023}, translated). This definition accounts for the uncertainty regarding the goal an \gls{AI} system should work towards, but also allows for a variety of systems to be considered \gls{AI}. Technically, every PID-controller and data processing software - see the work of \citeauthor{diplom} \autocite{diplom} for example - satisfies this definition of \gls{AI} systems. However, these systems are not what is referred to as \gls{AI} throughout this Thesis, instead the term describes \gls{AI} systems based on deep learning and natural language processing (see \autocite{aiHist_11202920}) that have been developed since the 2010s.
	
	\subsubsection{Large Language Model} \label{subsub:llm}
	At its core a Language Model is a \enquote{Transformer Language Model} \autocite{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels} as described by \citeauthor{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels}. It operates on small words or parts of words, which are called \glspl{token}.
	
	When a \gls{LLM} receives an input string, it divides it into \glspl{token} and feeds them to the transformation function. Based on these input \glspl{token} and the previous output \glspl{token}, the model calculates the most likely next output \gls{token}. The number of \glspl{token} a model can see, and therefore consider, is limited; the limit is called the \gls{context-window} of the model \autocite{howLLMwork}. The final result of the \gls{LLM} is its calculation for the most likely sequence of output \glspl{token} based on the given input, which means it provides the best result for the given goal, thereby fulfilling the definition of \gls{AI} (see \autoref{subsub:ai}). The process of developing capable \gls{LLM}s is beyond the scope of this Bachelor's Thesis.
	
	The distinction between Language Models and Large Language Models is in the number of internal parameters a model has. As stated in \citetitle{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels} \autocite{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels}, there is no hard cut-off for a Language Model to be considered Large. Commonly, models which are considered Large Language Models have $\geq 10 \cdot 10^{9}$ parameters \autocite{zhao2026surveylargelanguagemodels}.
	
	\subsubsection{Agentic \gls{AI}} \label{subsub:Agentic}
	As described by \citeauthor{agenticAiDefinitions}, Agentic \gls{AI}s are \enquote{autonomous, goal-driven systems that can operate on their own for long periods, requiring minimal human supervision} \autocite[3. Results]{agenticAiDefinitions}. In order to achieve this behaviour, these systems combine abilities which are beyond \gls{LLM}s. \citeauthor{agenticAiDefinitions} highlight the \enquote{strategic planning} \autocite[3. Results]{agenticAiDefinitions} capabilities, the preservation of the task's context over time \autocite{agenticAiDefinitions}, the usage of \enquote{external tools to extend [the system's] capabilities} \autocite[3. Results]{agenticAiDefinitions} and the collaboration of individual Agents (this means \gls{AI}-systems) with other Agents. \autocite{agenticAiDefinitions}
	
	\subsection{Decoding the Output of \gls{LLM}s}
	In \autoref{subsub:llm}, \gls{LLM} is defined based on the functionality it provides. In order to understand how the behaviour of \gls{LLM}s can be modified, a more formal definition is given, based on the work of \citeauthor{llmDetail} in \citetitle{llmDetail} \autocite{llmDetail}.
	
	\subsubsection{\gls{LLM} as a Function which Assigns Probabilities} \label{subsub:contextWindow}
	A \gls{LLM} can be described as a function $f$ with internal parameters $\theta$. Additionally, $f$ takes a set of input \glspl{token} $v_{in} = (i_1, ..., i_N)$, where $N \in \mathbb{N}$ is the number of input \glspl{token}, and a set of previous output \glspl{token} $v_{out,prev} = (o_{-1}, ..., o_{-M})$, where $M \in \mathbb{N}$ is the number of previous output \glspl{token}. The function $f(\theta, v_{in}, v_{out,prev})$ assigns a probability $s(v)$ to every \gls{token} $v \in \mathcal{V}$, where $\mathcal{V}$ is the vocabulary of \glspl{token} known to the \gls{LLM}:
	\begin{equation}
		f(\theta, v_{in}, v_{out,prev}) = \begin{pmatrix}
			s(v'_1) \\
			... \\
			s(v'_Z)
		\end{pmatrix} = s(v) \ \ \ \ \text{where $Z$ is the size of the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$} \label{LLM as function}
	\end{equation}
	$v'_i$ with $i \in [1,Z] \subset \mathbb{N}$ is an element from the ordered set $\mathcal{V}'$, which allows to map the rows of the probability matrix $s(v)$ to the \glspl{token} of the vocabulary.
	The probability indicates how well a \gls{token} is suited to be the next output \gls{token} \autocite[p.~4]{llmDetail}. The probabilities $s(v_i')$ are called \enquote{\glspl{logit}} \autocite[p.~4]{llmDetail}, they are not normalized by default and larger values of a \gls{logit} signal a better fit for the corresponding \gls{token} to be the next output \gls{token}. \autocite{llmDetail}
	
	The \gls{LLM} by itself outputs a probability distribution including every \gls{token} from its vocabulary. To choose a \gls{token} based on this probability distribution, \citeauthor{llmDetail} state that the following optimization problem needs to be solved, they label it the \enquote{MASTER PROBLEM} \autocite[p.~5]{llmDetail}:
	\begin{equation}
		q^*= \mathrm{arg}\max_{q \in \Delta(\mathcal{V})} \left[ \underbrace{{\langle q, s \rangle}}_{\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} q(v) \cdot s(v)} - \lambda \Omega(q)\right] \label{masterProblem}
	\end{equation}
	The used quantities are:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $q^*$ is the probability distribution maximizing \autoref{masterProblem},
		\item $\Delta (\mathcal{V})$ is the set of all possible probability distributions,
		\item $q$ is a single probability distribution for a given vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$,
		\item $s$ is the initial vector of \glspl{logit} the \gls{LLM} produces,
		\item $\Omega(q)$ is a regularisation function, which can push $q^*$ towards a certain distribution,
		\item $\lambda$ is the strength of the regularisation function, with $\lambda \geq 0$.
	\end{itemize}
	There are different approaches for finding $q^*$ and eventually getting a single \gls{token} from it, the ones relevant to this Thesis are introduced in the following.
	
	\subsubsection{Greedy Decoding} \label{subsub:greedy}
	This method uses no special regularisation function $\Omega(q)$, and therefore sets $\lambda=0$. This simplifies \autoref{masterProblem} to:
	\begin{equation}
		q^* = \mathrm{arg}\max_{q \in \Delta (\mathcal{V})}\langle q,s\rangle \label{eq:greedyDec}
	\end{equation}
	The resulting $q^*$ vector places all the probability mass on the \gls{token}, which was calculated to be most likely, while all other \glspl{token} get assigned a probability of 0. Therefore, the vector $q^*$ has the value 0 in every row except the one corresponding to the most likely \gls{token}, which has probability 1. This implies the next output \gls{token} is always the most likely \gls{token} according to the \gls{LLM}'s calculations. If multiple \glspl{token} have the highest probability $s(v)$, a single \gls{token} is selected among them. \autocite[p.~10]{llmDetail}
	
	\subsubsection{Softmax Decoding} \label{subsub:softmax}
	For Softmax Decoding, the chosen regularization function is $\Omega(q) = - \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} q(v) \log(q(v))$, which is the negative entropy of $q$. Using this $\Omega(q)$ in solving \autoref{masterProblem} leads to the optimal distribution $q^*$, where $q_t^*(v)$ is the probability for each \gls{token}: \autocite[p.~10-12]{llmDetail}
	\begin{equation}
		q_t^*(v) = \frac{\exp(\frac{s(v)}{\lambda})}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{V}}\exp(\frac{s(u)}{\lambda})}
	\end{equation}
	This distribution is normalised and uses the parameter $\lambda$, which controls the spread of the probability distribution $q^*$. For $\lambda > 1$, the probability is spread more evenly across all \glspl{token}, which results in previously unlikely \glspl{token} being more probable than before. When $\lambda < 1$, the probability distribution is sharper, with the probability mass being more concentrated around fewer \glspl{token} which already were likely before. The next output \gls{token} is selected according to the final probability distribution.
	For the edge case $\lambda = 0$, the regularisation function is disabled (see \autoref{masterProblem}), which causes the Softmax Decoding to become Greedy Decoding (see \autoref{subsub:greedy}). \autocite[p.~10-12]{llmDetail}
	
	The parameter $\lambda$ is one way of controlling the general randomness of the output. In the context of \gls{LLM}s, this parameter is often associated with the creativity of a model and called \textbf{Temperature} $T$. \autocite[p.~10-12]{llmDetail}
	
	\subsubsection{Top-k Decoding} \label{subsub:topk}
	Top-k Decoding extends Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) by selecting the $k$ ($k \in \mathbb{N} \setminus k = 0$) \glspl{token} with the highest \gls{logit} values \autocite[p.~12]{llmDetail}. These \glspl{token} form a subset of the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$ called $\mathcal{V}_k$ on which Softmax Decoding is performed, while the probability for every other \gls{token} $v \in \mathcal{V} \setminus \mathcal{V}_k$ is set to $0$.
	This gives the following probability distribution \autocite[p.~12]{llmDetail}:
	\begin{equation}
		q_t^*(v) = \begin{cases}
			\frac{e^{\frac{s(v)}{\lambda}}}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{V}_k}e^{\frac{s(u)}{\lambda}}} & \text{ for } v \in \mathcal{V}_k\\
			0 & \text{ else }\\
		\end{cases}
	\end{equation}
	For $k \geq \lvert\mathcal{V}\rvert$, Top-k Decoding is equivalent to the standard Softmax Decoding (see \autoref{subsub:softmax}). Setting $k = 1$ results in the same functionality as Greedy Decoding (see \autoref{subsub:greedy}). \autocite[p.~12]{llmDetail}
	
	From Top-k Decoding the parameter \textbf{top\_k} is derived, which can be used to shape the output of \gls{LLM}s \autocite[p.~12]{llmDetail}.
	
	\subsubsection{Top-p Decoding} \label{subsub:topp}
	Top-p Decoding is another extension to Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}), also selecting a subset of all \glspl{token} called $\mathcal{V}_p$ from the vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$. In contrast to Top-k Decoding (\autoref{subsub:topk}), Top-p Decoding defines a certain amount of probability $p \in \left(\right.0,1\left.\right]$, on which Softmax Decoding is performed. This is achieved by selecting the \gls{token} with the highest probability $s(v)$ from the set $\mathcal{V} \setminus \mathcal{V}_p$ and adding it to the set $\mathcal{V}_p$, thereby removing it from $\mathcal{V} \setminus \mathcal{V}_p$. This is repeated until the sum of the probabilities of all \glspl{token} $v \in \mathcal{V}_p$ exceeds the value of $p$. The probability of the \glspl{token} in $\mathcal{V}\setminus \mathcal{V}_p$ is set to $0$, while Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) is performed on the \glspl{token} in $\mathcal{V}_p$. The resulting probability distribution is (\autocite{llmDetail})
	\begin{equation}
		q_t^*(v) = \begin{cases}
			\frac{e^{\frac{s(v)}{\lambda}}}{\sum_{u \in \mathcal{V}_p}e^{\frac{s(u)}{\lambda}}} & \text{ for } v \in \mathcal{V}_p\\
			0 & \text{ else}\\
		\end{cases}.
	\end{equation}
	For $p=1$, Top-p Decoding is equivalent to Softmax Decoding, since all \glspl{token} are included in $\mathcal{V}_p$. For $p\rightarrow 0$, only the \gls{token} with the highest probability gets a non $0$ probability, which makes this case equivalent to Greedy Decoding (see \autoref{subsub:greedy}). \autocite{llmDetail}
	
	Top-p Decoding allows for dynamic adaptation based on how sharp or spread out the probability distribution initially returned by the \gls{LLM} is. For a sharp probability distribution, only the few very likely \glspl{token} are selected to the set $\mathcal{V}_p$ and Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) is performed on them. Whereas, for a flatter distribution, a higher number of \glspl{token} are included into the Softmax Decoding (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) and therefore are possible output \glspl{token}. \autocite{llmDetail}
	
	The derived parameter, called \textbf{top\_p}, is commonly used in the field of \gls{LLM}s to modify a system's output \autocite[p.~13]{llmDetail}.
	
	\subsection{Other Influences on an \gls{AI} System's Performance}
	As shown by numerous benchmarks (\autocite{huggArena}, \autocite{faceLeaderBoard}), different \gls{LLM}s achieve different performances. Some of the important factors for these differences are discussed in the following.
	\subsubsection{Predicting a \gls{LLM}'s Performance}
	One of the most noticeable differences in \gls{LLM}s is the number of internal parameters they use and the amount of data used in training the models. As shown by \citeauthor{powerLaw} \autocite[p.~5]{powerLaw}, combining the number of internal parameters $N$ a model uses and the number of \glspl{token} $D$ used in training a model can predict the \enquote{cross-entropy loss} \autocite[p.~6]{powerLaw} $L$ of a \gls{LLM}:
	\begin{equation}
		L(N,D) = \left[\left(\frac{N_C}{N}\right)^{\frac{\alpha_N}{\alpha_D}} + \frac{D_C}{D}\right]^{\alpha_D} \label{powerLawEq}
	\end{equation}
	The used quantities are:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $L(N,D)$ is the \enquote{cross-entropy loss} \autocite[p.~6]{powerLaw}. Lower values indicate a better model.
		\item $N_C$ is the critical number of internal parameters, until which \autoref{powerLawEq} is applicable. $N_C \approx 8.8 \cdot 10^{13}$ \autocite{powerLaw}.
		\item $N$ is the number of internal parameters the model uses.
		\item $D_C$ is the critical number of \glspl{token}, until which \autoref{powerLawEq} is applicable. $D_C \approx 5.4 \cdot 10^{13}$ \autocite{powerLaw}.
		\item $\alpha_N$ is the exponent for predicting $L(N)$. $\alpha_N \approx 0.076$ \autocite{powerLaw}.
		\item $\alpha_D$ is the exponent for predicting $L(D)$. $\alpha_D \approx 0.095$ \autocite{powerLaw}.
	\end{itemize}
	
	Other relevant influences on a \gls{LLM}'s performance are the number of training steps and the amount of \glspl{token} used per training step (batch size) \autocite{powerLaw}, as discussed by \citeauthor{powerLaw} \autocite{powerLaw}. \citeauthor{modelSelection} \autocite{modelSelection} show the importance of how the training data are composed, highlighting the increases in predicting a model's performance when considering this factor.
	
	\subsubsection{Tool Use of \gls{LLM}s} \label{subsub:tool}
	Since all \gls{LLM}s are trained on a finite dataset, every model has domains in which it lacks essential knowledge, limiting its ability to perform tasks in those areas effectively. To mitigate this, \gls{LLM}s are provided with additional information that enables them to answer the question at hand more reliably. In order to access external information, \gls{LLM}s are equipped with tools connecting to the programmed knowledge storage. As shown by \citeauthor{websearch1} \autocite{websearch1} as well as \citeauthor{websearch2} \autocite{websearch2}, equipping a \gls{LLM} with some sort of web-search tool reduces the amount of false outputs a system produces. Note that tool use is one of the features making an \gls{AI} system an \gls{Agentic AI} system, as discussed in \autoref{subsub:Agentic}.
	
	Another common class of tools are file readers, providing the ability to parse various formats like PDF, JSON, or plain text, thereby granting the \gls{LLM} invoking the tool access to external information. \autocite{crewaiTools}
	
	Code execution tools allow a \gls{LLM} to run generated code autonomously. This introduces the problem of untrusted code execution, since the model could generate malicious code, which accesses private information or modifies important data of the system the code is executed on. Therefore, untrusted code should only be executed inside a container separating the code from important data and systems. As stated by \citeauthor{sandboxEscape} \autocite{sandboxEscape}, these containers, called Sandboxes, are not always fully secure; strong \gls{LLM}s are able to escape them occasionally.

	\subsubsection{Reasoning} \label{subsub:reasoning}
	In the context of \gls{LLM}s, reasoning is creating a chain-of-thought, which means partitioning one big task into several smaller subtasks \autocite{reasoningGood}. A chain-of-thought improves the performance of \gls{LLM}s on tasks which require a multi-step process to obtain the correct solution, as shown by \citeauthor{reasoningGood} \autocite{reasoningGood}. 
	
	\subsection{Orchestrating \gls{AI}s to Behave as an \gls{Agentic AI}}
	As mentioned in \autoref{subsub:Agentic}, combining multiple \gls{AI} systems into one enables the resulting system to perform more complex tasks and achieve better results. The individual \gls{AI} systems of an \gls{Agentic AI} system are commonly referred to as \glspl{Agent}. In the following, the main approaches on how \glspl{Agent} can work together are presented, as they were outlined by \citeauthor{orchStyles} \autocite{orchStyles}.
	
	\subsubsection{Orchestration Architectures}
	\paragraph{The Sequential Pipeline} \autocite[p.~3]{orchStyles}\label{par:pipe} is a linear chain, where each \gls{Agent} is executed one after another. Individual \glspl{Agent} or their tools may be enabled to rerun, as long as the pipeline is still in the corresponding step. But once an \gls{Agent} is done, it can not be called again in the same run. The design process is relatively simple and the resulting systems show very deterministic behaviour, which allows errors to be located easily. \autocite{orchStyles}
	
	\paragraph{The Parallel Fan-Out with Merge} \autocite[p.~4]{orchStyles} \label{par:paral} differs from the Sequential Pipeline (\autoref{par:pipe}) by allowing certain \glspl{Agent} to run simultaneously. After the parallel \glspl{Agent} are done with their tasks, their results are merged by the next sequential \gls{Agent}. Introducing an \gls{Agent} before the parallelization allows for each parallel \gls{Agent} to only receive the information relevant to its task. Therefore, each parallel \gls{Agent} has to deal with less noise and receives less input \glspl{token}, which additionally contributes to stay within the \gls{LLM}'s \gls{context-window}. The \gls{Agent} responsible for merging resolves conflicts like contradicting information of parallel \glspl{Agent}. The parallel design allows for the highest throughput compared to the other architectures \autocite[p.~7]{orchStyles}. \autocite{orchStyles}
	
	\paragraph{The Hierarchical Supervisor-Worker} \autocite[p.~4-5]{orchStyles} \label{par:hierSup} architecture introduces a supervisor \gls{Agent}. It dynamically decides which \glspl{Agent} (Subagents) to call and which tasks each Subagent should handle. Furthermore, the supervisor \gls{Agent} reviews the Subagents' results and reruns the respective tasks, if it has low confidence in the result being correct. This forms a feedback loop, which allows the \gls{Agentic AI} system to dynamically assign more difficult tasks to more powerful \glspl{Agent}. The hierarchical architecture produces better results than the Sequential Pipeline (\autoref{par:pipe}) and the parallel design (\autoref{par:paral}), but is also more costly regarding the execution time \autocite[p.~7]{orchStyles}. \autocite{orchStyles}
	
	\paragraph{The Reflexive Self-Correcting Loop} \label{par:RefLoop} \autocite[p.~5]{orchStyles} \label{par:reflex} executes the \glspl{Agent} in a predefined order similar to the Sequential Pipeline (\autoref{par:pipe}), but adds a verification step. The verification is performed by an \gls{Agent}, which either deems the result satisfactory and declares it to be the system's output, or decides it requires improvement. Before the result is reintroduced into the system, it is critiqued by stating the problems encountered, which allows for targeted corrections on the next cycle. This architecture refines the output over time rather than simply rerunning the entire system. The Reflexive Self-Correction Loop \autocite[p.~5]{orchStyles} achieves the highest accuracy but also comes at the highest cost of all architectures discussed \autocite[p.~7]{orchStyles}. \autocite{orchStyles}
	
	When interpreting the performances of the architectures discussed, it should be taken into account that \citeauthor{orchStyles} \autocite[p.~14-15]{orchStyles} tested them on their extraction capabilities from financial documents.
	Therefore, the performances could change in different domains and for different tasks. When designing \gls{Agentic AI} systems, the combination of multiple architectures is possible.
	
	\subsubsection{Communication Inside \gls{Agentic AI} Systems} \label{subsub:protocols}
	Communication protocols are used to define how the different components of an \gls{Agentic AI} system interact. An orchestration framework may implement some or all of these protocols or similar ones. The \gls{LLM}s are wrapped into a layered architecture, which handles the interactions between different \gls{LLM}s and manages how tools are called. \autocite{howToolsAreUsed}
	
	\paragraph{The Model Context Protocol (MCP)} \autocite{orchFrameworks} (documentation: \autocite{MCP}) handles how the underlying \gls{LLM} of an \gls{Agent} uses tools.
	
	\paragraph{The Agent Communication Protocol (ACP)} \autocite{orchFrameworks} (documentation: \autocite{ACP}) allows different \glspl{Agent} to communicate with each other. It is based on a client-server architecture and uses \enquote{RESTful} communication \autocite{protocols}.
	
	\paragraph{The Agent-to-Agent Protocol (A2A)} \autocite{orchFrameworks} (documentation: \autocite{A2A}) handles \gls{Agent} interactions to enable effective cooperation. This is achieved by \glspl{Agent} communicating their capabilities to each other \autocite{protocols}.
	
	\paragraph{The Agent Network Protocol (ANP)} \autocite{orchFrameworks} (documentation: \autocite{ANP}) manages decentralised communication between \glspl{Agent}. It allows for secure interactions over the internet \autocite{protocols}.
	
	\subsubsection{Memory} \label{subsub:memory}
	In the context of \gls{Agentic AI}, memory is an \gls{Agent}'s awareness of previous actions and contents. Short-term memory includes the context of the task the \gls{Agent} is currently performing. Long-term memory allows an \gls{Agent} to access information it obtained during previous runs \autocite{orchFrameworks}. Other kinds of memory include \enquote{semantic memory} \autocite{orchFrameworks}, \enquote{procedural memory} \autocite{orchFrameworks}, and \enquote{episodic memory} \autocite{orchFrameworks}, but these are beyond the scope of this Thesis.
	
	\subsection{Evaluation of \gls{AI} Systems} \label{sub:eval}
	In order to measure the influence of an \gls{AI} system's configurations, a metric for the systems performance is required. One approach is testing the \gls{AI} system on a set of multiple choice questions, with each answer being clearly right or wrong \autocite{mmlu}. The total score over the set of questions indicates the system's performance. This method is used for the \enquote{GPQA Diamond} benchmark \autocite{gpqaDiamondPaper} and partly for the \enquote{Massive Multi-discipline Multimodal Understanding and Reasoning (MMMU)} \autocite{MMMU} benchmark, which are both part of the \citeauthor{artAnaLeaderboard} \citetitle{artAnaLeaderboard} \autocite{artAnaLeaderboard}.
	
	Another option are open answer questions, which require a written response by the tested model. This method is used by \enquote{SciBench} \autocite{scibench}, where the answers usually are short and need to match the expected answer exactly, since a string comparison of them is performed. The performance over all questions is accumulated and provides a final evaluation score of the tested system. \autocite{scibench}
	
	In order to properly assess an \gls{AI} system's capability to handle more complex tasks, human grading can be employed. This is done by Arena Intelligence \autocite{arena}, where users write a prompt and pick the best from two anonymous responses. Human grading is also used in \enquote{SciEx} \autocite{humanGrading}, where experts evaluate the \gls{LLM}'s results. As acknowledged by \citeauthor{humanGrading} \autocite{humanGrading}, human grading is not feasible for large-scale benchmarking due to its time and resource inefficiency.
	
	\subsubsection{CritPt} \label{subsub:critPt}
	Presented by \citeauthor{critPtArx}, \enquote{CritPt} \autocite{critPtArx} introduces checkpoints which allow to identify a model’s progress on a question. This provides a more in‑depth view of the failure points of the evaluated system. The probed system is prompted with a question and generates an answer for it; afterwards, the required output format must be adopted to enable automatic evaluation. \citeauthor{critPtArx} describe using the checkpoints to guide the system towards the correct answer and also mention a mechanism which assists the tested system in adopting the required output format. \autocite{critPtArx}
	
	There are three types of questions, which require either a numerical value, a symbolic expression, or executable Python code. Composite answers are only graded as correct if all individual parts are correct. \autocite{critPtArx}
	
	CritPt tests a system on 70 problems which are publicly available via their GitHub repository \autocite{critptGit}, the displayed scores are averages over five runs of the benchmark. The answers to the problem sets are not made public to prevent models from finding them on the internet, rendering the benchmark useless. Therefore, the model's answers to the questions are submitted to an evaluation server, returning the final result of the benchmark. Users are limited to 10 submissions per 24 hours, in order to prevent leakage of the correct answers. For the same reason, no solutions or attempts at them should be publicised. \autocite[p.~10]{critPtArx}

	\section{Design of the Physics Modelling Assistant}
	In order to easily enable future work, the Physics Modelling Assistant uses open-source and free-of-charge services whenever feasible.
	
	\subsection{Orchestration Framework: CrewAI} \label{sub:crewAi}
	The decision was made not to design a new framework for orchestrating \gls{AI}s in order to save time on this software development heavy task and instead focus on the Physics Modelling Assistant's configurations. \citeauthor{orchFrameworks} \autocite{orchFrameworks} discuss a number of frameworks varying in complexity and beginner friendliness. For this Thesis, the CrewAI \autocite{crewAi} framework is used for its role focused style \autocite{orchFrameworks}, consisting of \glspl{Agent} and Tasks \autocite{crewAi} which provide a level of abstraction from the technical details of the orchestration. Every \gls{Agent} can be configured individually, which includes using different \gls{LLM}s for each \gls{Agent}. Every Task has a description and specifications regarding the output and the output format, along with the \gls{Agent} designated to it \autocite{taskPy}. On execution of a Task, its instructions are merged with the assigned \gls{Agent}'s, providing the input for the underlying \gls{LLM}. An \gls{Agent}'s behaviour is defined by its role, goal, and backstory. The role is a very short description comparable to a job title. The goal specifies the actions an \gls{Agent} is supposed to perform similar to a job description. Details for the \gls{Agent}'s behaviour are provided via the backstory argument, including preferences or advice on how to approach the Task. CrewAI allows to access any \gls{LLM}, provided the user presents a valid \gls{API key} for the model. The \gls{LLM} can be assigned parameters including the discussed Temperature (\autoref{subsub:softmax}) and top\_p (\autoref{subsub:topp}), the number of \glspl{token} to be used, and others. Tools can be assigned to both \glspl{Agent} and Tasks, with CrewAI providing ready-to-use tools, but also allowing to add custom tools \autocite{crewaiTool}.

	The organisation of multiple CrewAI \glspl{Agent} and Tasks is managed by the Crew \autocite{crewaiCrew}. As depicted in \autoref{fig:crewExe}, it is responsible for initializing and validating the \gls{Agent}'s and Task's configurations before launching the system. As stated by \citeauthor{orchFrameworks} \autocite{orchFrameworks}, CrewAI's orchestration combines elements from the ACP, A2A and ANP (see \autoref{subsub:protocols}). The CrewAI documentation \autocite{crewAi} and the source code available on GitHub \autocite{crewPy} show a sequential and a hierarchical execution approach. The sequential execution (see \autoref{fig:crewExe}) runs the Tasks following the order prescribed by the source code, thereby forming a sequential Pipeline (see \autoref{par:pipe}). This can be modified into a Parallel Fan-Out with Merge (see \autoref{par:paral}) by allowing certain Tasks to run at the same time, which CrewAI calls asynchronous \autocite{crewaiParallel}. Merging is handled by a specific Task, waiting for all parallel Tasks to be done before starting its work (see Flows \autocite{crewaiFlow}). The hierarchical execution style (see \autoref{fig:crewExe}) of a CrewAI Crew enables a Hierarchical Supervisor-Worker architecture (see \autoref{par:hierSup}) by delegating Tasks, or aspects of them, to Subtasks. Implementing a Reflexive Self-Correcting Loop (see \autoref{par:reflex}) with CrewAI can be achieved by making use of Flows \autocite{crewaiFlow}, which allow for conditional calls and the coupling of multiple Crews.
	
	In order to achieve a deterministic workflow and allow for easy adjustments to the setup, the Sequential Pipeline architecture (\autoref{par:pipe}) is used for the Physics Modelling Assistant.

	Since the addition of DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 to the GWDG service, it is the highest performing of the provided models across all benchmarks considered in \autoref{saiaModelsBench}, significantly outperforming all other models at CritPt with a score of \si{17 \percent}. It also offers the largest \gls{context-window} at $1\,$million \glspl{token}.

	\subsection{Tools Used}
	To enhance the Physics Modelling Assistant's capabilities, certain \glspl{Agent} are equipped with tools (see \autoref{subsub:tool}). Each tool provides an object specifying the expected input and its constraints to the \gls{Agent} using the tool.
	
	\subsubsection{arxiv\_paper\_tool with Exponential Backoff} \label{subsub:arxivPaperTool}
	CrewAI already provides the \enquote{arxiv\_Paper\_Tool} \autocite{crewaiArxivPaperTool}, enabling \glspl{Agent} to access the arXiv-API \autocite{arxiv} for finding papers relevant to the question at hand. During the development process of the Physics Modelling Assistant, the CrewAI tool's requests repeatedly kept getting denied, resulting in no papers being fetched and downloaded from arXiv. As stated in the arXiv-API's Terms of Use \autocite{arxivTermsOfUse}, rate limits are enforced, blocking clients from sending many requests in a short amount of time. Furthermore, web servers deny repeated requests from the same client for the same service \autocite{rechnernetze}. Since the CrewAI arxiv\_paper\_tool \autocite{crewaiArxivPaperTool} does not implement any strategy for waiting, its requests are eventually blocked by the arXiv-API servers. As a consequence the arxiv\_paper\_tool was extended by implementing an exponential back-off strategy, as it is common for modern server requests \autocite{rechnernetze}. Additionally to the exponential back-off, the modified tool, called arxiv\_paper\_tool\_exponential\_backoff, added a small random wait, which is common to prevent two clients requesting the same service from waiting for the same amount of time, thereby blocking each others requests repeatedly \autocite{rechnernetze}. The tool finds a specified number of papers and downloads them to a designated directory.
	
	\subsubsection{pdf\_reader} \label{subsub:pdfReader}
	In order to access the papers downloaded from arXiv, a PDF reader tool was developed. It is able to read all PDF files from the specified directory, allowing the invoking \gls{Agent}'s \gls{LLM} to see the contents of the documents. In theory, this allows the system to read sources of any number and length from the directory. In practice, however, every \gls{LLM} has a context window, which limits the amount of \glspl{token} it can process. When the number of \glspl{token} read exceeds the \gls{context-window}, the \gls{Agent} fails its Task.
	
	Since the tool only reads files from a hard-coded directory, it is deemed secure to be used outside of a Sandbox environment (see \autoref{subsub:tool}).
	
	\subsubsection{dimensional\_analysis} \label{subsub:dimAna}
	During the testing of the Physics Modelling Assistant, formulas from the downloaded sources were stated accurately, but assigning correct units to the individual quantities proved to be difficult for the responsible \gls{Agent}. Mistakes in the unit assumptions lead to unphysical results or impossible units for the outcomes of the numerical implementation. To verify the unit consistency for all formulas used, the dimensional\_analysis tool was created. It is based on the SymPy library \autocite{symPy} and allows an \gls{Agent} to check whether the units it proposes are consistent for the relevant formulas. The tool receives an equation written as a string, a dictionary containing the used quantities and their proposed units, and a list of all units that are used. Inside the tool the units are substituted in for their respective quantities, before both sides of the formula are simplified separately. Numerical values are not considered, since the aim is only to achieve unit consistency. Lastly, the left side of the formula is divided by the right side, resulting in the unit correction required on the right side for the formula to achieve consistency. If this value is $1$ then no correction is needed, otherwise the \gls{Agent} invoking the tool may apply corrections to the assumed units of the used quantities to correct the mismatch. Another conclusion the \gls{Agent} may draw is to modify the tested formula by inserting physical constants which are otherwise absorbed by differing definitions of the used quantities. This allows to work with real world units while implementing a formula, which uses a different unit system.
	
	\subsubsection{Code Execution}
	As discussed by \citeauthor{sandboxEscape} \autocite{sandboxEscape}, a secure Sandbox is complex to design but necessary if \gls{LLM} generated code is executed directly. As stated in \autoref{subsub:tool}, the weak points of Sandboxes can be exploited by capable \gls{LLM}s, which is why the Physics Modelling Assistant omits code execution.
	
	\subsection{Experimental Setup for the Pipeline} \label{sub:expSetup}
	The final system is designed to mirror a simplified human research workflow consisting of finding relevant information, developing a model based on the knowledge acquired, and implementing the model to provide insight into its predictive capabilities. In order to properly answer the questions from benchmarks (see \autoref{sub:eval}), the final Task is responsible for providing the output in the benchmark's required format. The recently introduced DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 \gls{LLM} is not considered for this setup for the reasons discussed in \autoref{sub:LlmUsed}.
	
	A simplified view of the Physics Modelling Assistant's steps is shown in \autoref{fig:tasks}, each step is performed by one Task and its corresponding \gls{Agent}.
	
	\subsubsection{Input} \label{subsub:input}
	The Physics Modelling Assistant receives the problem description, a string called \textit{aim}, and instructions for the required format, likewise as a string. Additional parameters may be set via an interface, but default values are provided. The output directory specifies where the Tasks store their generated files; its name consists of the question number and a unique identifier, ensuring previous outputs are not overwritten. The Temperature (see \autoref{subsub:softmax}) and the top\_p (see \autoref{subsub:topp}) parameters can be modified to shape the \gls{LLM}'s output, along with the max\_tokens parameter limiting the number of \glspl{token} a \gls{LLM} produces \autocite{saiaModels}.
	
	Each \gls{Agent} can perform reasoning before executing its assigned Task. Reasoning implies that an \gls{Agent} creates a plan for how to approach the Task before working on it \autocite{crewaiAgentDoc} (see \autoref{subsub:reasoning}). The maximum number of reasoning attempts can be specified; otherwise the \gls{Agent} retries to create a plan until an attempt is deemed sufficient \autocite{crewaiAgentDoc}. An \gls{Agent} is also able to rerun itself to improve its output if it determines that further improvement is required. The maximum number of iterations is set through the max\_iter parameter \autocite{crewaiAgentDoc}, the default is 20.
	
	\subsubsection{General Parameter Choices} \label{subsub:paramCh}
	Since the GWDG webpage recommends Temperature and top\_p values for all three \gls{LLM}s considered, these defaults are retained, as they are the solutions to the optimization problem discussed in \autoref{subsub:contextWindow}, and changes would disturb the ideal configuration. For GLM 4.7 and Qwen3.6 27B the recommended parameters are Temperature = 1.0 and top\_p = 0.95; the suggested Temperature for Qwen3.5 397B is lower at 0.6. For the reasons discussed in \autoref{subsub:buildModel}, the Qwen3.5 397B model is not used in the system, which allows Temperature and top\_p to be configured globally, as seen in \autoref{tab:defPar}.
	
	The maximum number of output \glspl{token} is set to \si{50 \kilo} (see \autoref{tab:defPar}) to ensure that the \gls{context-window} is not exceeded. Qwen3.6 27B's \gls{context-window} enables it to handle a maximum of \si{262\kilo} \glspl{token}, while Qwen3.5 397B A17B and GLM 4.7 have a \gls{context-window} of \si{256\kilo} and \si{200\kilo} \glspl{token} respectively \autocite{saiaModDetail}. The advantages of accommodating a large number of input \glspl{token} need to be balanced with the number of output \glspl{token} being sufficient for the \gls{LLM} to state its results.
	
	As discussed in \autoref{subsub:reasoning}, an \gls{Agent} performing reasoning is generally desirable for it tends to yield improved results on complex challenges. The superiority of feedback loops mentioned in \autoref{par:RefLoop} indicates a positive effect of an \gls{Agent} rerunning itself. However, more reasoning attempts and reiterations increase the computation effort, with all retries counting towards the rate limit shown in \autoref{tab:rateLimit}. Therefore, both parameters are set to 3 (see \autoref{tab:defPar}), allowing for modest dynamic improvements while minimizing the strain on the rather tight rate limit.
	
	With one run of the Physics Modelling Assistant using eight \glspl{Agent} (see \autoref{fig:tasks}) and each of them using all possible reasoning attempts and reiterations, a total of 48 requests are sent to the \gls{LLM}s, with every request counting towards the providers rate limits stated in \autoref{tab:rateLimit}.
	
	The Physics Modelling Agent does not use dedicated memory (\autoref{subsub:memory}) but relies on CrewAI passing the outputs of previous Tasks to later ones via a shared output object (see \autocite[line 1610]{crewPy}).

	\subsubsection{Obtaining Information} \label{subsub:obtainInfo}
	\paragraph{Source Gathering} \label{par:srcGath}
	In order to obtain sufficient information for building a model, the arXiv-API \autocite{arxiv} is accessed via the modified arxiv\_paper\_tool (see \autoref{subsub:arxivPaperTool}) and the papers retrieved are downloaded. The corresponding \textbf{source gatherer} \gls{Agent} is based on the Qwen3.6 27B \gls{LLM} and is tasked with using its tool for finding papers related to the aim. This model is used due to its superior performance in long context reasoning (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}), which may also include searching for relevant information. The \textbf{gathering task} specifies that three to six papers should be found, with more papers being downloaded bearing the risk of exceeding the \gls{context-window} of the \gls{LLM}s used in following steps. The Task further specifies which information should be listed for each source: arXiv ID, author, date, URL, and a short summary. This content is written into a Markdown report, allowing a human to inspect the sources found.
	
	This \gls{Agent} does not use reasoning as its Task is deemed to not require a multi step approach. All remaining parameters are kept at the global settings listed in \autoref{tab:defPar}.
	
	Because of the issues with the arXiv-API limits \autocite{arxiv}, this Task is packaged into its own Crew, allowing it to be executed independently of all later steps. This way, changes or reruns intended to alter the behaviour of later Tasks can be made without rerunning the arXiv request again. For the evaluation of the Physics Modelling Assistant's performance in this Thesis, source gathering is executed on every retry, to provide a final result reached in one continuous run for every CritPt challenge.
	
	\paragraph{Information Extraction} \label{par:infoExtr} By knowing the directory the downloaded papers are saved to, the \textbf{information extractor} \gls{Agent} can invoke its pdf\_reader tool (see \autoref{subsub:pdfReader}) to obtain the contents of the files in that directory. The Qwen3.6 27B \gls{LLM} is used for its superior long context reasoning performance (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}), along with its \gls{context-window} of \si{262 \kilo} \glspl{token} being the largest among the considered models.
	
	The \textbf{information extractor} is instructed to find the information required to build a model that accomplishes what the aim asks for. It is specified to present its findings with proper citation and its backstory emphasises to stay true to the provided source material. The focus on stating the used sources is chosen to allow a human to verify the information stated by the \gls{LLM}. The corresponding \textbf{extraction task} specifies the output to be in Markdown format, enforcing an unambiguous and human readable output.
	
	The parameters are kept at the default settings stated in \autoref{subsub:paramCh}.
	
	\subsubsection{Building the Model} \label{subsub:buildModel}
	\paragraph{Modelling} \label{par:Modelling}
	For building the model which is at the heart of the Physics Modelling Assistant, the \gls{LLM} strongest in scientific and specifically physics reasoning should be used. Therefore, the first choice was Qwen3.5 397B A17B, which performed the best at scientific reasoning (GPQA Diamond \autocite{gpqaDiamondPaper}) and joined best at CritPt \autocite{critPtArx}, as shown in \autoref{saiaModelsBench}. During the testing phase, requests to this model repeatedly failed with an answer not being provided by the server before a 30 minute timeout was reached. For that reason the GLM 4.7 \gls{LLM} is used instead, for it being the joined second best model at scientific reasoning (GPQA Diamond \autocite{gpqaDiamondPaper}) and joined best model at CritPt \autocite{critPtArx} (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}). The \textbf{modeller} \gls{Agent} is prompted to provide a mathematical description for the model it builds to accomplish the aim. It is further instructed to rely on the information provided by the previous extraction Task, provide a proper explanation for the steps taken, and not to write any code. The last instruction targets the \gls{Agent}'s shown behaviour of occasionally writing code which already implemented the model. This is not wanted due to potential modifications required for the model to be sufficient.
	
	The \textbf{modelling task} defines the expected output to be a complete mathematical description of the model along with textual explanations. The requested format is once again Markdown, ensuring human readability of the generated output file. The default parameter settings described in \autoref{tab:defPar} are adopted.
	
	\paragraph{Unit Check} \label{par:unitCheck}
	The \textbf{unit checker} \gls{Agent} accounts for domain specific parameter definitions and other conventions preventing a straightforward implementation of the model and its formulas. Since this requires genuine reasoning capabilities, the GLM 4.7 \gls{LLM} is chosen. The goal specifies the following steps:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item The units of all the quantities should be determined.
		\item The assumed units should be checked with the dimensional\_analysis tool (see \autoref{subsub:dimAna}) to identify potential inconsistencies of the current suggestion.
		\item Based on the tool's results, necessary corrections to the proposed units or the current formulas may be applied.
	\end{enumerate}
	The \textbf{unit checking task} specifies the units of all quantities to be displayed in the Markdown output file along with the results of the tool calls made. The parameter configuration aligns with the default (see \autoref{tab:defPar}).
	
	\paragraph{Parameters Suggestion} \label{par:ParamSug}
	In order to obtain a meaningful simulation, physically realistic starting conditions are required. The \textbf{parameter suggester} is asked to provide them along with derivations for its suggestions, justifying the use of the GLM 4.7 \gls{LLM} for its reasoning capabilities. The \textbf{parameter suggestion task} emphasizes that explanations for the parameter choices are supposed to be included in the Markdown output file. The input parameters are unchanged compared to the defaults from \autoref{tab:defPar}.
	
	\subsubsection{Implementing the Model} \label{subsub:implMod}
	\paragraph{Physics Simulation} \label{par:physicSim}
	In order to capture the suggested model accurately, the \textbf{simulation physician} \gls{Agent} is run with the goal of implementing the formulas of the model precisely, without making changes to them. The \gls{Agent} is based on the GLM 4.7 \gls{LLM}, since it performs the best in coding and science among the considered models (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}). As specified by the \textbf{physician simulation task}, the implementation is supposed to use the corrected units and formulas determined by the unit checking task (see \autoref{par:unitCheck}). To obtain human readable and interpretable results, the Task is requested to create graphics if it deems this appropriate. The output is specified to be a Python file containing executable code, with explanations added as comments. The parameters remain the same as before (see \autoref{tab:defPar}).
	
	\paragraph{Simulation Correction} \label{par:simCorrection}
	Since the previous physician simulation task is meant to capture the model's logic into working code, it is not asked to prioritize code quality. The \textbf{simulation correcter} is tasked to correct coding mistakes and improve the code's efficiency and style, while maintaining the implemented formulas. The corresponding \textbf{simulation correction task} defines the expected output to be working Python code. The parameter configurations and \gls{LLM} are unchanged compared to the simulation physician described in \autoref{par:physicSim}.
	
	\subsubsection{Giving a Final Answer} \label{subsub:finalAnswer}
	Benchmarks like CritPt expect a comprehensive answer (see \autoref{sub:eval}) to their questions, which is why the \textbf{final outputer} \gls{Agent} is appended to the pipeline. Its goal is to provide a complete answer that obeys the requested format, which is crucial for CritPt's automated grading process \autocite{critPtArx}. The \textbf{final output task} is asked to generate an output file in Markdown format. The GLM 4.7 model is used for its performance on the CritPt benchmark stated in \autoref{saiaModelsBench} and the issues with Qwen3.5 397B A17B discussed in \autoref{par:Modelling}.
	
	\subsubsection{Output} \label{subsub:outputBackticks}
	The result of the Physics Modelling Assistant is an object containing a string with the final Task's result, representing the answer to the defined aim. The output files generated by each Task can be inspected by a human; the Python scripts are not directly executable since they contain triple backticks at the beginning and end. Presumably, this is done to mark the section in between as code, which is not valid Python syntax. Removing or commenting the backticks results in the scripts becoming executable, apart from possible package installations.

	\subsubsection{Connecting to CritPt's Benchmark}
	CritPt is based on the inspect\_ai package \autocite{inspectAI_UK_AI_Security_Institute_Inspect_AI_Framework_2024}, which allows to register a model by implementing the ModelAPI interface. This is achieved by the crafted PMAModelAPI class, exposing the required generate method, which receives the challenge description from CritPt, but not its number or the required output format. This does not align with the statement from \autoref{subsub:critPt}, suggesting the system described by \citeauthor{critPtArx} \autocite{critPtArx} is modified compared to the one publicly available on GitHub \autocite{critptGit}. The same is true for CritPt guiding the system towards the correct result via checkpoints. The 70 publicly available challenges do not include any checkpoints and the GitHub repository does not mention how to obtain them \autocite{critptGit}.
	
	The PMAModelAPI instance is programmed to identify the current challenge based on the problem description it receives, allowing it to obtain the challenge number and the required output format. 
	
	Due to conflicting dependencies of the inspect\_ai and crewai packages regarding incompatible versions of the tiktoken and pydantic packages, the PMAModelAPI instance calls the Physics Modelling Assistant as a subprocess running in a different virtual environment. From the superprocess, the subprocess receives the problem description, the requested output format, and the challenge number along with a unique identifier. The latter allows for the processes to communicate via writing to and reading from a shared file, without it being overwritten by other runs of the system. This was implemented to work around the problem of extracting the Physics Modelling
	Assistant’s output from the standard output (std\_out) of the subprocess, which also contains all content that would be displayed on the terminal. Furthermore, the PMAModelAPI provides the subprocess with the values for the parameters as stated in \autoref{tab:defPar}.
	
	The subprocess consists of two layers. The top layer handles the communication with the superprocess and triggers the bottom layer. This is the Physics Modelling Assistant which works through the steps described in \autoref{sub:expSetup} and returns its final output string to the top layer. With the superprocess waiting for the subprocess to finish, the output is written to the agreed file before completion is reported.
	
	The superprocess obtains the output from the specified file and forwards the result to CritPt, which stores it inside a JSON-file along with some metadata. The complete set of JSON-files can be send to the evaluation server via a script provided by CritPt \autocite{critptGit}. Since Artificial Analysis \autocite{artifAna} has integrated CritPt into their ecosystem, a valid \gls{API key} from Artificial Analysis \autocite{artifAna} is required for the evaluation server. It is free of charge and can be obtained by creating an account on Artificial Analysis. For the evaluation requests to be approved, the account connected to the submitting \gls{API key} needs to be marked as a trusted research account by Artificial Analysis \autocite{artAnaTrusted}. For the work presented in this Thesis, presenting a valid \gls{API key} enabled the necessary evaluations to be made, without explicit approval being required.
	
	\subsection{Investigating the Physics Modelling Assistant's Performance} \label{sub:invPerf}
	Due to the limited access to the \gls{LLM}s described in \autoref{tab:rateLimit}, only two full sets of answers are generated and evaluated for each configuration, instead of the five normally used for benchmarking systems with CritPt (\autoref{subsub:critPt}). This aims to balance the desire to test multiple configurations, with the need to obtain statistical results, and the constraints imposed by the limited access to the used \gls{LLM}s.
	
	\subsubsection{An Example Run} \label{subsub:exRun}
	To provide insight into the Physics Modelling Assistant's capabilities, the system is given an example modelling request. It is not taken from the CritPt benchmark, since no solutions or attempts of the same may be publicised (see \autoref{subsub:critPt}). The following aim is chosen:
	
	\enquote{Calculate the Edelstein effect for a Rashba fermion (at the Gamma point of the Brillouin zone). \newline
	Compute the magnetization magnitude and direction of different directions and magnitudes of the applied electric field. \newline
	Consider how the result depends on relevant parameters of the model (e.g. chirality, Fermi velocity, spin-orbit coupling strength) and make explicit graphics.}
	
	The final output is specified to be \enquote{simple Markdown format}.
	The parameters have the default values listed in \autoref{tab:defPar}.
	
	This setup aims to showcase how the Physics Modelling Assistant could be used in an actual work environment, where more open end questions are explored. In contrast to that, benchmark questions (see \autoref{sub:eval}) often require a single short answer which is either correct or incorrect.
	
	The individual Tasks in this setup are expected to behave according their configurations specified in \autoref{subsub:obtainInfo} to \autoref{subsub:finalAnswer}. To enable a clearer and more measurable way of assessing each Task's behaviour, several key criteria are introduced.
	
	\paragraph{Criteria for Obtaining Information} \label{criteria:info}
	For the source gathering Task, it is important that the number of papers downloaded is within the set boundaries of three to six. The information contained in the output file is evaluated based on whether it provides the required bibliographic details needed to identify each paper. All downloaded papers must be included in the report without omissions or additions, and each paper must be relevant to the specified aim.
	
	When extracting information from the downloaded sources, citations must be provided for every stated fact. Each source must genuinely contain the information attributed to it. All content written to the output file should be relevant for constructing the model.
	
	\paragraph{Criteria for Building the Model} \label{criteria:model}
	The modelling step needs to state every formula required for performing calculations with the model. It is supposed to derive additional formulas if needed and correctly cite any information that was not derived.
	
	For every formula which is checked, the assumed unit are supposed to be stated. When verifying an expression, the Task and its \gls{Agent} are evaluated on using the tool and interpreting its results correctly. In the end, all properties should have correct units with regard to the formulas they are used in.
	
	The suggested parameters are expected to suit the described model. The individual choices need to be supported or justified.
	
	\paragraph{Criteria for Implementing the Model} \label{criteria:implement}
	The physics simulation task is responsible for capturing the model's logic into code. All formulas and parameters should be used according to the previous findings. The code is required to produce graphics if this is directly requested, otherwise it is optional.
	
	After the simulation correction, all implemented formulas and there parameters are supposed to retain their previous form. Corrections should be applied to fix errors in the code and possibly improve its style, resulting in an executable script, except from the manual removal of the backticks described in \autoref{subsub:outputBackticks}.
	
	\paragraph{Criteria for Giving a Final Answer} \label{criteria:answer}
	The output format is to be implemented correctly, providing the Physics Modelling Assistant's result for the given aim.
	
	\subsubsection{The Pipeline's Performance Compared to Individual Models} \label{subsub:Reference}
	The Physics Modelling Assistant's performance in the default setup (see \autoref{sub:expSetup}) is measured against the CritPt benchmark (see \autoref{subsub:critPt}). Its results are compared to the performances of the individual \gls{LLM}s used for the system.
		
	\subsubsection{The Effect of the Context-Window Length} \label{subsub:1Deep}
	The default setup is modified to use the DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 model instead of the Qwen3.6 27B model for the \gls{Agent} assigned to the extraction Task (see \autoref{par:infoExtr}). Both models achieve similar scores on long context reasoning (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}), but at $1\,$million \glspl{token}, DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731's \gls{context-window} is almost four times larger than Qwen3.6 27B's. The achieved performance of this configuration is compared to the reference setup described in \autoref{sub:expSetup}.
	
	\subsubsection{The Performance when Using DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731} \label{subsub:allDeep}
	The newly added DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 model is used for every \gls{Agent} of the Physics Modelling Assistant, for the model's superior performance across all considered benchmarks (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}). This configuration's performance is compared to the other setups described in \autoref{subsub:Reference} and \autoref{subsub:1Deep}.

	\section{Results and Discussion}	
	\subsection{Results of the Example Run from the Reference Setup} \label{sub:exRunRes}
	\subsubsection{Results for Obtaining Information} \label{results:Info}
	\paragraph{Source Gathering} \label{par:src}
	A total of six papers were downloaded from arXiv (see \autoref{appendix:src}). The Markdown report includes their titles, along with bibliographic information, and short summary. For the full report is available on GitHub \autocite{gitSources}.
	
	This Task worked according to the defined criteria \autoref{criteria:info}, staying within the imposed limit of downloading a maximum of six papers and creating a report that contains the required bibliographic information for just the downloaded papers. All sources directly relate to the Edelstein effect and discuss aspects of it which are relevant to the modelling request (see \autoref{subsub:exRun}).
	
	\paragraph{Extracted Information} \label{pma:extrInfo}
	The report on this Task includes a few additional sources. A variety of formulas are stated and explained, starting with the Rashba Hamiltonian
	\begin{equation}
		\hat{H} = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \alpha\,\hat{z}\cdot(\mathbf{p}\times\boldsymbol{\sigma}) \text{,}
	\end{equation}
	where $\alpha$ is the Rashba coupling strength and $\sigma$ are the Pauli-matrices. A more detailed description can be found in \autoref{appendix:exInfo}. Other information regarding energy and spin properties are stated, before analytical results for the magnetization are presented:
	\begin{align}
		\text{HDR} \ \ \ \ \ M_y &= \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\,m\alpha\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y\\
		\text{LDR} \ \ \ \ \ M_y &= \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\,\sqrt{m^2\alpha^2 + 2mE_F}\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y
	\end{align}
	Additionally, the Edelstein susceptibility and an anisotropic case are discussed, along with the parameter dependencies of some key formulas. Instructions and code for the implementation are provided as well. More details can be found in the appendix (\autoref{appendix:exInfo}) or on GitHub \autocite{gitInfo}.
	
	All of the additional sources stated are real scientific publications. The provided information are properly referenced to the correct papers as intended. Apart from the implementation guidelines, the provided formulas and descriptions match the intended Task output, although some of the provided information is not essential to the question at hand and its inclusion appears to be influenced by the downloaded papers discussing them. To improve the results, providing code should be discouraged, since the implemented formulas are not yet validated and therefore may be inconsistent.

	\subsubsection{Results for Building the Model} \label{results:model}
	\paragraph{Modelling} \label{pma:model}
	Since the modelling Task restated the extracted information described in \autoref{pma:extrInfo}, its output is not provided in more detail, but it can be accessed via GitHub \autocite{gitModel}. If the file appears not to be displayed correctly on GitHub, it might be due to the used Markdown viewer. During the work on this Thesis, \citetitle{mdView} \autocite{mdView} was used, providing a clean human readable look except for incorrect Markdown code.
	
	The modelling step kept the correct citations from the information extraction (see \autoref{pma:extrInfo}). The provided information being restated point to the Task and its \gls{Agent} either relying too much on the information found, or deeming it sufficient for answering the modelling request. Considering the Tasks output for the benchmarking questions described in \autoref{pma:benchRes}, the latter appears to be the case. Contrary to the criteria stated in \autoref{criteria:model}, some formulas used in following steps (see \autoref{pma:sim}) were not stated or derived. This applies to the expression used for distinguishing between the high density regime (HDR) and low density regime (LDR). A possible explanation for the Task deeming the information stated by the extraction Task sufficient is the broad phrasing of the modelling request (\autoref{subsub:exRun}), emphasizes general dependencies to be discussed.
	
	\paragraph{Unit Check} \label{pma:unitCheck}
	The key formulas were checked for dimensional consistency, with the assumed units being stated at the start of every individual check. From the Rashba Hamiltonian, the Rashba coupling strength $\alpha$ was derived to have units of velocity (see \autoref{appendix:unit}). A factor of $\frac{1}{\hbar^2}$ was added to formula for the magnetization, resulting in
	\begin{align}
		\text{HDR} \ \ \ \ \ M_y &= \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi\, \hbar^2}\,m\alpha\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y \label{HDRMag}\\
		\text{LDR} \ \ \ \ \ M_y &= \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi \hbar^2}\,\sqrt{m^2\alpha^2 + 2mE_F}\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y \label{LDRMag}
	\end{align}
	For other formulas like the Edelstein susceptibility, no tool invocations are mentioned and no unit system was determined. More detailed information are provided in the appendix (\autoref{appendix:unit}) and on GitHub (\autocite{gitUnit}).
	
	The checked formulas are essential for the model and for the Rashba Hamiltonian and the magnetization formula, the applied corrections lead to a consistent unit system (see \autoref{appendix:unitVer}). This was achieved, although the tool was not used as intended. Instead of simplifying the properties' units/dimensions to base units/dimensions, the \gls{Agent} invoked the tool with more complex dimensions, like \textit{magnetization} or \textit{electric field}. Since the dimensional analysis tool (see \autoref{subsub:dimAna}) does not break the provided units down, but only performs algebra on them, the tool's output did not offer the expected correction. Therefore, improvement is necessary to enable the \gls{Agent} to use its tool correctly. Providing a full usage example or explicitly stating that base units are required, appear to be promising attempts.
	
	Since no tool invocations were recorded when working on th formulas for the Edelstein susceptibility and the consistency of the initially proposed units could neither be verified nor disproven, this part of the unit check is deemed a failure.
	
	\paragraph{Parameter Suggestions} \label{pma:par}
	This Task generally acted according to its specifications, providing numeric values for each parameter relevant to the model and justifying its suggestions. However, the units determined by the previous Task were not adopted for the effective mass and Rashba coupling strength. For every suggested parameter, a justification was provided, they can be examined in \autoref{appendix:paramSug}. The full report is provided at \autocite{gitParams}.
	
	The suggested parameters are mostly fine, except for the Rashba Coupling strength, which did not adopt the units determined by the unit check (see \autoref{pma:unitCheck}). Theses results suggest an over-reliance on using starting parameters from other sources, which is why more emphasize on adapting the found parameters to the corrected formulas from the unit check (see \autoref{pma:unitCheck}) is required. The provided justifications contain substantial mistakes, which are discussed in \autoref{appendix:paramSug}. Additionally, two of the three sources stated to support the claims state incorrect bibliographic information. To prevent the \gls{LLM} from hallucinating non existent papers, enabling the corresponding \gls{Agent} to use the modified arxiv\_paper\_tool (see \autoref{subsub:arxivPaperTool}) for finding real papers appears to be a viable option, since the source gathering (see \autoref{par:srcGath}) did not have any hallucinations when using this tool.

	\subsubsection{Results for Implementing the Model} \label{results:imple}
	\paragraph{Physics Simulation} \label{pma:sim}

The full code can be inspected at \autocite{gitImp}. An additional expression for determining whether the system is in the HDR or LDR was implemented, although it was not stated by the modelling Task (see \autoref{pma:model}). The code creates graphics for visualizing the parameter dependencies of the implemented formulas.
	
	The generated code implements the corrected formulas for the HDR (see \autoref{HDRMag}) and LDR magnetization (see \autoref{LDRMag}). The units used for the parameters do not align with the findings of the unit check (\autoref{appendix:unit}). Instead, the suggestions for the starting parameters (see \autoref{appendix:paramSug}) Task were adopted. The resulting inconsistencies reflect the issues encountered with finding suitable starting parameters, rather than suggesting a fundamental flaw with adapting the model into working code. Graphics highlighting the model's parameter dependencies are generated as requested. 
	
	\paragraph{Simulation Correction} \label{pma:simCor}

	The final output mostly reiterated the extracted information (see \autoref{pma:extrInfo}). For the full Markdown report, see \autocite{gitFin}.

	\subsubsection{Discussion of the Results from the Example Run}
	In its current configuration, the Physics Modelling Assistant properly downloads related sources from arXiv for the given input. It extracts relevant information and is able to apply unit corrections to extracted formulas, although struggling with using the tool for the unit check properly. Failures occur when finding realistic starting parameters and implementing the extracted formulas, with the identified unit corrections not being considered. The final Task providing an answer to the specified aim is only relevant if a concrete question and answer format are provided, not for a broad modelling request. 
	
	Future work should address the system not using the tool for checking units correctly, thereby not yielding meaningful results from it. The configurations for the parameters suggestion Task and \gls{Agent} should emphasize the necessity of providing realistic starting parameters in the previously determined units.
	
	The general design, yet simple, is applicable for providing relevant information and implementing basic visualizations for a given request. To assess its ability of contributing to actual research, the results from the CritPt benchmarking need to be taken into account.
	
	\subsection{Evaluation of the Physics Modelling Assistant}
	\subsubsection{Issues Encountered during the Generation} \label{res:issues}
	The most common reason for failure was the API rate limit of SAIA \autocite{saiaModels} being exceeded, resulting in a 429 error code. Rarely, the \gls{context-window} of a \gls{LLM} was breached, resulting in failed runs. Lastly, the adoption of the output format was inconsistent. As stated in \autoref{subsub:outputBackticks}, the backticks and the word \enquote{python} needed to be removed, which was accomplished via an automated script. However, some results included additional descriptions not marked as comments in front of the actual output, requiring further post generation corrections to establish the proper format. On a few occasions, the output format was not adopted at all, with only a Markdown-like descriptions being returned. If the post processing was unable to obtain output of the correct format, the challenge was retried, which also includes challenges failing due to the previously mentioned issues.

	The SAIA rate limit itself can not be adjusted, as it is fixed by the GWDG. The remaining number of requests left may be checked before a run of the system is begun, to prevent attempts which can not complete from starting in the first place. Since the number of \gls{LLM} calls is not fixed but depends on possible reruns of an \gls{Agent} being invoked, the number of \gls{LLM} usages needed can only be estimated. The possible downsides of additional \gls{LLM} calls are comparably small. Considering the current maximum amount of 48 requests being made per run (see \autoref{subsub:paramCh}) and the rate limit of 30 calls per minute (see \autoref{tab:rateLimit}), a single incomplete run causes a maximum additional waiting time of two minutes. Therefore, gain of tracking the current rate usage is negligible unless the Physics Modelling Assistant is evaluated at a substantially larger scale than in this Thesis.

	Failures because of the \gls{context-window} being exceeded on the extraction Task (see \autoref{par:infoExtr}) are preventable when the number of input \glspl{token} is controlled. By dividing PDF files with too many \glspl{token} into multiple chunks, single files can not exceed the \gls{context-window} any more. Then the extraction \gls{Agent} needs to be run multiple times, only reading an amount of \glspl{token} the underlying \gls{LLM}'s \gls{context-window} can accommodate. To achieve this behaviour, the Physics Modelling Assistant needs to dynamically adjust how many times the information extraction Task is run.

	\subsection{Benchmarking Results for the Default Setup} \label{benchmarking:default}
	Two independent generation runs were conducted and evaluated. As shown in \autoref{tab:pmaBench}, the accuracies achieved on the evaluation are \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{2.86}{\percent} for the first run and \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{0.00}{\percent} for the second. Since only two sets of answers were evaluated, the results are highly uncertain and more independent evaluation runs are required before a definitive result is obtained. The values returned by CritPt have a higher precision than the stated results, therefore, the first were used for calculating the average and sample standard deviation. The mean accuracy of \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{1.43(2.02)}{\percent} suggests that the Physics Modelling Assistant does not offer an improved performance compared to the underlying \gls{LLM}s used, which are Qwen3.6 27B and GLM 4.7, scoring at \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=0]{1}{\percent} and \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=1]{1.7}{\percent} respectively.
	
	The individual behaviour of the Tasks on the benchmarking questions differs from the one observed for a modelling request as described in \autoref{sub:exRunRes}. Fewer sources are found and the information extracted is more condensed. Contrary to the behaviour described in \autoref{pma:model}, the modelling Task carries out new calculations based on the information provided, working towards solving the challenge, whereas the unit check only applies few corrections and deems most formulas consistent. Since CritPt provides explicit numerical values for the described problem \autocite{critptGit}, the Task suggesting parameters does not contribute in a meaningful way. Although not in the required format, the two coding Tasks implement the requested calculations, but also additional code visualizing the results. The answer Task is mostly able to adopt the required output format, although post generation processing is often required to remove additional content like textual descriptions or calculations not obeying the answer function required by the CritPt imposed output format.
	
	\subsection{Benchmarking Results when Using DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 for Extracting Information} \label{benchmarking:deep1}
	For the Crew using DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 instead of Qwen3.6 27B for the information extraction Task (see \autoref{par:infoExtr}), the achieved accuracies of \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{0.00}{\percent} and \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{1.43}{\percent} result in an average accuracy of \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{0.71(1.01)}{\percent} (see \autoref{tab:pmaBench}) when using the sample standard deviation to obtain the uncertainty. Since only two evaluations were conducted, these results are interpreted as an indicative range for the performance rather than the definitive benchmarking score for this setup. For the individual Task, a similar behaviour as discussed in \autoref{benchmarking:default} was observed, supporting the claims that the stated improvements are necessary. Therefore, neither the \gls{context-window} length nor the \gls{LLM} used for extraction information influence the performance significantly.
	
	The score obtained does not suggest a significant influence of using the DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 instead of the Qwen3.6 27B \gls{LLM} on the information extraction Task. Taking the accuracy of the default setup (see \autoref{benchmarking:default}) into account, these findings align with the statements from the CritPt developers, suggesting that a web-search offers no substantial improvement on the CritPt challenges \autocite[p.~10]{critPtArx}.
	
	It appears to be possible that the models used are lacking some of the basic knowledge required for some of the benchmarking questions and neither a regular web-search nor arXiv papers provide the required knowledge. Enabling the information extraction to access a set of physics textbooks may provide more insight, but requires the adjustments discussed in \autoref{res:issues} to deal with the limited \gls{context-window}. Building on this idea, the system could access a retrieval argument generation (RAG) database, providing a broader selection of sources. As of \DTMDate{2026-08-15}, among the SAIA models only E5 Mistral 7B Instruct (see \autocite{saiaModDetail}) explicitly performs embedding, which means turning a string into a vector which allows to search for similar ones in a vector database. Since is has a \gls{context-window} of only 4096 \glspl{token} \autocite{saiaModDetail}, it is not feasible for encoding sources into vectors. Therefore, an external RAG database needs to be accessed or a new one has to be developed.
	
	\subsubsection{Benchmarking Results when Using DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 for Every \gls{Agent}} \label{benchmark:allDeep}
	When using DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 as the underlying \gls{LLM} for every \gls{Agent} of the Physics Modelling Assistant, accuracies of \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{0.00}{\percent} and \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{0.00}{\percent} were achieved, as seen in \autoref{tab:pmaBench}. The resulting average accuracy and sample standard deviation of \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{0.00(0.00)}{\percent} is not sufficient to provide a final benchmarking score and is only used as an indication for the systems capabilities. Since the uncertainty is calculated from only two measurements, a value comparable to the previously found \SI{2.02}{\percent} is estimated, resulting resulting in a precision of \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=2]{0.00(2.02)}{\percent}. With regard to the uncertainty, this configuration's performance does not differ from the other setups discussed in \autoref{benchmarking:default} and \autoref{benchmarking:deep1} significantly. Compared to the previously use Qwen3.6 27B and GLM 4.7 models both scoring less than \SI{2}{\percent}, DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 performs much higher on the CritPt benchmark at \SI{17}{\percent} (see \autoref{saiaModelsBench}).
	
	The performance degradation observed for CritPt when using the DeepSeek model for the Physics Modelling Assistant compared to using this model without any orchestration framework points to a negative effect of the Physics Modelling Assistant for on the \gls{LLM}'s capability to handle CritPt challenges. The exact failure points can not be determined from the work done in this Thesis, but an educated guess is possible. The overall design of the Physics Modelling Assistant aims at building and implementing models based on sources, not to solve a concrete question. This is reflected in the systems architecture visualized in \autoref{fig:tasks}, using three of its eight Tasks for working towards implementing a proposed model. Finding information related to the problem before starting to tackle it is reasonable when building a scientific model. But for the CritPt challenges, a web-search does not provide a substantial boost in performance, as shown by the CriPt developers \autocite[p.~10]{critPtArx} and indicated by the findings of this Thesis stated in \autoref{benchmarking:deep1}. Therefore, the Tasks responsible for finding sources and extracting information are not crucial for tackling the CritPt challenges. The modelling Task being instructed to reference its findings from the extracted information is not was needed for the CritPt benchmark either. Overall no Task of the Pipeline developed for this Thesis is suited particularly well for performing the precise calculations required. To significantly improve the system's performance, Tasks aiming to implement a model should be removed as well as the reliance of informations from the found sources. Additionally, Tasks for validating and refining the results may be added before the final output is constructed. Implementing these modifications would most certainly break the Physics Modelling Assistant's desired behaviour to provide and implement a model (see \autoref{sub:exRunRes}) based on a broad request as seen in \autoref{subsub:exRun}.
	
	If both tackling concrete challenges and crafting more general models is desired, the current Pipeline architecture is most likely insufficient due to its unadaptability to different kinds of tasks. A more flexible architecture is required, allowing the system to dynamically decide which steps are necessary for achieving the desired result and invoking only those. The \enquote{Hierarchical Supervisor-Worker} architecture \autocite[p.~4-5]{orchStyles} as described by \citeauthor{orchStyles} \autocite{orchStyles} and presented in \autoref{par:hierSup}, is able to provide the required adaptability. As discussed, has one main \gls{Agent} deciding which Subagents are assigned which parts of the given task.

	\subsection{Additional Remarks about Benchmarking} \label{pma:benchRes}
	For the reasons discussed in \autoref{sub:invPerf}, this Thesis does not provide a definitive CritPt benchmarking score for the Physics Modelling Assistant, since for no configuration five independent generations and evaluations were conducted.
	
	The times for a complete run were inconsistent, with some runs succeeding in generating output in the correct format taking less than five minutes, while other challenges took over an hour to complete. There are many factors influencing the execution time, ranging from the capabilities and usage of the system running the Physics Modelling Assistant to the time it takes the server providing the \gls{LLM}s to process the request. For that reason, the times recorded throughout different runs do no provide meaningful insight into the system but rather into the environment the system lives in.

	\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
	The stated example run suggests that the Physics Modelling Assistant is generally capable of building a simple model based on arXiv \autocite{arxiv} papers found. The model's implementation requires few manual corrections and provides a visualization. When benchmarked against CritPt \autocite{critPtArx}, the system shows no significant improvements compared to the results of the individual \gls{LLM}s it used internally and appears to degrade the performance of the DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731 model, pointing to the Physics Modelling Assistant's ineffectiveness for orchestrating \gls{LLM}s. To verify this tendency, the system should be benchmarked with a full five runs instead of the two conducted for each setup during this Thesis.
	
	Future improvements of the Physics Modelling Assistant should address the recorded struggles of the system to use its tool for checking the dimensional consistency of the model's formulas, as well as implementation of the model towards adopting the proposed units for the quantities used.
	
	Future research may include a variety of tests regarding the Physics Modelling Assistant's configurations. Instead of enabling an arXiv search, a selection of physics textbooks could be provided, offering a more structured and complete knowledge foundation compared to research papers. To accommodate large inputs like textbooks, the current mechanism for reading sources needs to be modified, to prevent the \gls{context-window} from being exceeded.	The influence of the number of reasoning attempts and reiteration attempts the \glspl{Agent} are allowed to make can be investigated. As stated by \citeauthor{reasoningGood} \autocite{reasoningGood}, reasoning has a positive effect on a system's performance; the better performances of architectures allowing for refinement compared to those which do not \autocite{orchStyles} suggest that reiterations are beneficial as well. These tendencies could be systematically explored for the Physics Modelling Assistant to map the influences of reasoning and reiterations in more detail.	To investigate the influence of configuration changes on the Physics Modelling Assistant's purpose to craft and implement models based on scientific sources, a measurement for their quality is needed to elevate such research beyond anecdotal evidence.
	
	If the Physics Modelling Assistant is to be used effectively in a scientific work, improvements beyond modifications to the source finding and model implementation are required. The current pipeline architecture provides a very predictable behaviour but restricts the system's adaptability to different kinds of problems, which is why a more flexible design is required. To achieve this, elements of the \enquote{Hierarchical Supervisor-Worker} \autocite[p.~4-5]{orchStyles} and \enquote{Reflexive Self-Correcting Loop} \autocite[p.~5]{orchStyles} architectures should be implemented. This idea is supported by existing examples of \gls{Agentic AI} systems like the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern} and \enquote{SciSciGPT} \autocite{sciSci}. Both of these systems dynamically decide which steps are taken towards achieving the current goal. They also implement feedback mechanisms, based on the indicated confidence in the results a rerun of one or multiple steps is conducted. Contrary to the Physics Modelling Assistant, the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern} and \enquote{SciSciGPT} \autocite{sciSci} employ less specialized \glspl{Agent}, enabling them to operate on broader and more complex tasks. The superiority of this architecture is backed by the CritPt benchmarking \autocite{critPtArx} results of the \enquote{physics-intern}, recording improvements of up to \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=1]{13.4}{\percent} in absolute and \SI[round-mode=places, round-precision=1]{267.5}{\percent} in relative numbers \autocite{pIntern}. To account for possible effects regarding specific \gls{LLM} and \gls{Agentic AI} system combinations, testing the models used for the Physics Modelling Assistant on the \enquote{physics-intern} \autocite{pIntern} and vice versa may be conducted.
	
	In its current state, the Physics Modelling Assistant is more useful as a prototype rather than as a helpful assistant for a human scientist. The design process and the obtained results provide some insight into the design of \gls{Agentic AI} systems, pointing to the importance of a feedback architecture which is supported by the results of \citeauthor{orchStyles} \autocite{orchStyles}. The simple design used is a reasonable starting point for systematically investigating the influences of parameters on systems combining multiple \glspl{LLM}. By implementing a pipeline architecture, the current state of the Physics Modelling Assistant provides an interesting addition to the current landscape of \gls{Agentic AI} frameworks for science, allowing to compare the different architectures in more detail.

	\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, breakable, title=The Extracted Rashba Hamiltonian as Stated in the Output,colback=blue!5!white, colframe=blue!75!black] \label{pma:hamilton}
		The \textbf{Rashba Hamiltonian} for a 2DEG at the $\Gamma$ point is [Bychkov \& Rashba, \textit{JETP Lett.} 39, 78 (1984)]:
		
		$$
		\boxed{\hat{H} = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \alpha\,\hat{z}\cdot(\mathbf{p}\times\boldsymbol{\sigma})}
		$$
		
		where:\begin{itemize}
			\item[-] $m$ is the effective carrier mass,
			\item[-] $\alpha$ is the Rashba spin–orbit coupling (SOC) strength,
			\item[-] $\mathbf{p} = \hbar\mathbf{k}$ is the momentum,
			\item[-] $\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z)$ are the Pauli matrices,
			\item[-] $\hat{z}$ is the direction perpendicular to the 2D plane.
		\end{itemize}
	\end{tcolorbox}
	Most of the core findings are attributed to the work of \citeauthor{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025} \autocite{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025}, including the \textit{energy dispersion}, the \textit{spin eigenstates}, and the \textit{Fermi wavenumbers} split into high density regime (HDR) and low density regime (LDR). The general formula for the magnetization, which is stated to be the total spin density instead of the \textbf{magnetization density}, is referenced to both \autocite{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025} and \autocite{paper2_Ezawa_2025}. The following analytical results fo the isotropic case are provided:
	\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, breakable, title=Analytical Magnetization as Stated in the Output, colback=blue!5!white, colframe=blue!75!black] \label{pma:magn}
		\textbf{\Large4.1 Magnetization in the High-Density Regime (HDR)}
		
		For an electric field $\mathbf{E} = E_x\hat{x}$, the spin density along $\hat{y}$ in the HDR (both bands occupied) is [Gaiardoni et al., arXiv:2503.20712 (2025)]:
		
		$$
		\boxed{M_y = \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\,m\alpha\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y}
		$$
		
		\textbf{Key result:} In the HDR, the magnetization is \textbf{constant, independent of the Fermi energy} $E_F$, and proportional to the Rashba SOC parameter $\alpha$ and the effective mass $m$.\newline
		
		\textbf{\Large4.2 Magnetization in the Low-Density Regime (LDR)}
		
		In the LDR (only the lower band occupied) [Gaiardoni et al., arXiv:2503.20712 (2025)]:
		
		$$
		\boxed{M_y = \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\,\sqrt{m^2\alpha^2 + 2mE_F}\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y}
		$$
		
		For small $E_F$ near the band crossing, this can be expanded as:
		
		$$
		M_y \approx \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\left(\alpha m + \frac{1}{2}\frac{E_F}{\alpha}\right)[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y
		$$
		
		The spin density \textbf{increases linearly with the Fermi energy}. In the limit $\alpha \to 0$, the second term vanishes because $E_F$ has a quadratic dependence on $\alpha$.
	\end{tcolorbox}
	Further information include the \textit{Anisotropic Rashba Model}, the \textit{Out-of-Plane Edelstein Effect}, the \textit{Acoustic Edelstein Effect}, and the \textit{Effect of a Magnetic Field}. The parameter dependencies are discussed for both the magnetization $M$ and the Edelstein susceptibility $\chi_{xy}$. Lastly, the Task provides guidelines for a numerical implementation along with code snippets and graphics it suggests to be implemented. The full report is available on GitHub at \autocite{gitInfo}
	
	\subsubsection{Unit Check} \label{appendix:unit}
	\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, breakable, title=Unit Check of the Rashba Hamiltonian as Stated in the Output, colback=blue!5!white, colframe=blue!75!black] \label{pma:unitHam}
		\textbf{\Large Formula}
		$$
		\hat{H} = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \alpha\,\hat{z}\cdot(\mathbf{p}\times\boldsymbol{\sigma})
		$$
		
		\textbf{\large Quantities and Units}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item[-] $H$ (Hamiltonian): Energy $\rightarrow [E]$ (Joules)
			\item[-] $p$ (Momentum): $[p] = [M][L][T]^{-1}$
			\item[-] $m$ (Mass): $[m] = [M]$
			\item[-] $\alpha$ (Rashba parameter): $[\alpha] = ?$
			\item[-] $\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ (Pauli matrices): Dimensionless
		\end{itemize}
		
		\textbf{\large Tool Input}
		\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!10!white]
			equation: H = p**2 / (2*m) + alpha * (p)\\
			dimensions: {"H": "energy", "p": "momentum", "m": "mass", "alpha": "alpha\_dimension"}\\
			unitList: energy, momentum, mass, alpha\_dimension
		\end{tcolorbox}
		
		
		\textbf{\large Tool Output}
		\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!10!white]
			2*energy*mass/(momentum*(2*alpha\_dimension*mass + momentum))
		\end{tcolorbox}
		
		\textbf{\large Analysis}
		The first term $\frac{p^2}{2m}$ has units $\frac{([M][L][T]^{-1})^2}{[M]} = [M][L]^2[T]^{-2} = [E]$.
		The tool output shows that for consistency, \textit{alpha\_dimension} must satisfy \textit{energy = momentum * alpha\_dimension}.
		Since $[E] = [M][L]^2[T]^{-2}$ and $[p] = [M][L][T]^{-1}$, we must have:
		$$
		[\alpha] = \frac{[E]}{[p]} = \frac{[M][L]^2[T]^{-2}}{[M][L][T]^{-1}} = [L][T]^{-1}
		$$
		\textbf{Conclusion:} The Rashba parameter $\alpha$ has units of \textbf{velocity} ($m/s$)
	\end{tcolorbox}
	The energy dispersion is deemed dimensionally consistent by the tool output and no correction is applied. For the analytical magnetization formula, the \gls{Agent} describes its process as follows:
	\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced, breakable, title=Unit Correction for the HDR Magnetization as Stated in the Output, colback=blue!5!white, colframe=blue!75!black] \label{pma:unitHDR}
		\textbf{\large Formula}
		$$
		M_y = \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi}\,m\alpha\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y
		$$
		For dimensional analysis, we examine the product $M_y \propto \mu_B e \tau m \alpha E$.
		
		\textbf{\large Quantities and Units}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item[-] $M_y$ (Magnetization/Spin Density): Magnetic moment per unit volume.
			\item[-] In SI (Volume): $[M] = [I][L]^2$ (Am$^2$) / $[L]^3$ = $[I][L]^{-1}$ (A/m).
			\item[-] In 2D (Area): $[M] = [I]$ (Amperes) / $[L]^2$ = $[I][L]^{-2}$ (A/m$^2$).
			\item[-] In microscopic units (just density): $[M] \propto [\hbar]^{-1}[L]^{-2}$.
			\item[-] $\mu_B$ (Bohr magneton): $[\mu_B] = [I][L]^2$ (Am$^2$ or J/T).
			\item[-] $e$ (Elementary charge): $[e] = [I][T]$.
			\item[-] $\tau$ (Relaxation time): $[\tau] = [T]$.
			\item[-] $m$ (Mass): $[m] = [M]$.
			\item[-] $\alpha$ (Velocity): $[\alpha] = [L][T]^{-1}$.
			\item[-] $E$ (Electric field): $[E] = [M][L]^3[T]^{-3}[I]^{-1}$ (V/m).
		\end{itemize}
		
		\textbf{\large Tool Input}
		\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!10!white]
			equation: M\_y = mu\_B * e * tau * m * alpha * E\newline
			dimensions: {"M\_y": "magnetization", "mu\_B": "magnetic\_moment", "e": "charge", "tau": "time", "m": "mass", "alpha": "velocity", "E": "electric\_field"}\newline
			unitList: magnetization, magnetic\_moment, charge, time, mass, velocity, electric\_field
		\end{tcolorbox}
		
		\textbf{\large Tool Output}
		\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!10!white]
			magnetization*exp(-1)/(charge*magnetic\_moment*mass*time*velocity*electric\_field)
		\end{tcolorbox}
		...omitting a long analysis which is part of the original output...\\
		
		\textbf{Conclusion on Formula Consistency:}
		The formula is dimensionally consistent \textbf{if and only if} the system of units uses $\hbar=1$ (natural units) or the parameters $m$ and $\alpha$ are defined in units that include $\hbar$ (common in computational materials science, e.g., $\alpha$ in $eV\cdot\mathring{A}$ and $m$ in $eV^{-1}\mathring{A}^{-2}\cdot \hbar^2$).
		However, in standard SI units, the formula is missing a factor of $\hbar^{-2}$.
		
		\textbf{Correction for Standard Units:}
		$$
		M_y = \frac{\mu_B |e|\tau}{2\pi\hbar^2}\,m\alpha\,[\hat{z}\times\mathbf{E}]_y
		$$
	\end{tcolorbox}

Additionally, formulas for the anisotropic susceptibility and the Out-of-Plane susceptibility are analysed, with the output not stating that tool calls were made during the process and failing to provide a unit system for them. Lastly, the findings are summarised, restating the $\frac{1}{\hbar^2}$ correction for both HDR and LDR magnetization.

	\subsubsection{Verification of the Unit Check for the Magnetization Magnitude} \label{appendix:unitVer}
	This unit check correctly identifies the Rashba coupling strength $\alpha$ to be a velocity for the given Hamiltonian (see \autoref{pma:unitHam}). The unit correction applied to the magnetization formulas achieves unit consistency, although some steps along the way are worth being discussed. The assumed units for the magnetization are not correct: The extracted information (see \autoref{appendix:exInfo}) state that $M_y$ is the total spin density, which is also incorrect, since the magnetization is the magnetic moment (\si{\ampere m^2}) per unit volume. The formula is referenced to \citeauthor{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025} \autocite{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025}, who discuss a 2D system, which has a unit volume with dimensions of Length$^2$. Therefore, the unit for the magnetization is $\frac{\si{\ampere \meter^2} }{\si{\meter^2}} = \si{\ampere}$.
	
	In the following, the correction applied to the magnetization by the Physics Modelling Assistant is verified by hand. The used properties are the following:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $M_y$ is the magnetization (\si{\ampere \meter^2}) per 2D unit volume, resulting in its unit being \si{\ampere}.
		\item $\mu_B$ is the Bohr magneton. It is expressed in \si{\joule \tesla^{-1}}.
		\item $e$ is the charge with the unit of \si{\ampere \second}.
		\item $\tau$ is the transport time expressed in \si{\second}.
		\item $m$ is the effective mass in \si{\kilogram}.
		\item $\alpha$ is the Rashba coupling strength. As stated in \autoref{pma:unitHam}, it has units of velocity being \si{\meter\second^{-1}}.
		\item $\hat{z}$ is a dimensionless direction.
		\item $\mathbf{E}$ is the applied electric field. It can be expressed in \si{\volt\meter^{-1}}.
		\item $\hbar$ is the Planck constant with SI units of \si{\joule \second}.
	\end{itemize}
	The relevant unit conversions are
	\begin{align}
		1 \si{\tesla} &= 1 \frac{\si{\volt \second}}{\si{\meter^2}} \label{con:tesla} \text{  and} \\
		1 \si{\joule} &= 1 \si{\kilogram} \frac{\si{\meter^2}}{\si{\second^2}} \label{con:joule} \textbf{.}
	\end{align}
	This allows for the following derivation steps: 
	\begin{align} \label{pma:unitHand}
		\left[M_y\right] &\overset{!}{\hat{=}} \left[\mu_B |e| \tau m \alpha \left(\hat{z} \times \mathbf{E}\right)_y\frac{1}{2 \pi \hbar^2}\right] \notag\\
		\si{\ampere} &\overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\frac{\joule}{\tesla} \cdot \ampere \second \cdot \second \cdot \kilogram \cdot \frac{\meter}{\second} \cdot \frac{\volt}{\meter} \cdot \frac{1}{\joule^2 \second^2}} \notag\\
		& \text{now use \autoref{con:tesla}:} \notag\\
		&\overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\frac{\joule \meter^2}{\volt \second} \cdot \ampere \second \cdot \second \cdot \kilogram \cdot \frac{\meter}{\second} \cdot \frac{\volt}{\meter} \cdot \frac{1}{\joule^2 \second^2}} \notag\\
		&\overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\frac{\joule \meter^2}{\volt \second} \cdot \ampere \second \cdot \second \cdot \kilogram \cdot \frac{\meter}{\second} \cdot \frac{\volt}{\meter} \cdot \frac{1}{\joule^2 \second^2}} \notag\\
		&\overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\ampere \frac{\kilogram \meter^3 \second^2}{\joule \second^4 \meter}} \notag\\
		&\overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\ampere \frac{1}{\joule} \cdot \kilogram \frac{\meter^2}{\second^2}} \notag\\
		& \text{now use \autoref{con:joule}:} \notag\\
		&\overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\ampere} \ \ \text{ verifies unit consistency}
	\end{align}
	If $\left[\alpha\right] = \mathrm{eV}\si{\angstrom} \overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\joule \meter}$ as stated in \citetitle{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025} \autocite{paper1_Gaiardoni_2025}, an additional $\frac{1}{\hbar}$ correction is required, resulting in $\left[\frac{\alpha}{\hbar}\right] \overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\frac{\joule \meter}{\joule \second}} \overset{!}{\hat{=}} \si{\frac{\meter}{\second}}$.

	\subsubsection{Implementation and Simulation} \label{appendix:impl}
	The two simulation Tasks create two functionally identical files with only minor differences regarding the comments. On execution the code calculates the Rashba effect induced change $E_R$ of the minimum energy. It is compared to the Fermi energy to determine if the system is in the high density regime (HDR) or low density regime (LDR), with the Fermi energy being higher than $E_R$ indicating the HDR. The code's output is a graphic showing various dependencies for the Rashba effect. To access the unmodified code, see \autocite{gitImp}. It is executable but requires some corrections. 
	The code uses the Rashba coupling strength in \si{\joule \meter}, but does not apply the additional correction of $\frac{1}{\hbar}$ (see \autoref{appendix:unitVer}). Therefore, the initial results were off by this factor, with a magnetization $M_y$ in the range of $10^{-44}$ $\frac{\si{\ampere}}{\si{\meter}}$. The unit for the magnetization was also incorrect; as stated in \autoref{appendix:unit}, it should be \si{\ampere}. To obtain more meaningful results, the following modifications were applied by hand:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item the magnetization formulas for both HDR and LDR got an additional $\frac{1}{\hbar}$ factor,
		\item the axis labels for the unit of the magnetization $M_y$ was changed to Am\^{}2/m\^{}2,
		\item the axis labels for the unit of the Rashba coupling strength $\alpha$ were fixed from \textit{eV$\cdot$$\backslash$AA} to \textit{eV$\cdot$A}.
	\end{itemize}
	The resulting graphic is shown in \autoref{fig:simFig}. The corrected code is accessible via \autocite{gitCor}.
